# Equity and Co-founders

> Based on mainland China company and tax law as generally applied; rules get revised. For anything touching shareholding ratios, articles of association, holding vehicles or tax, have a local lawyer and tax adviser check it before you act. The material behind this card was written against the earlier version of the company law and tax policy in force at the time, so treat the percentages, tax rates and thresholds below only as clues to which points to verify.

The costliest mistakes between co-founders often come at the very start: the first split becomes the reference point for every later negotiation, and as the business changes, so does who contributes most. At the same time, a shareholding percentage is not the same as a say in decisions — the articles of association, agreements and holding vehicles can all separate voting rights from capital contributed, each at a price. This card takes three steps: first get past the problem of the split hardening, then write adjustment and exit into rules agreed in advance, and finally work out which shareholding line you are on and which control tools you have.

## Quick take

- **Jurisdiction**: Based on mainland China company and tax law as practised before the revised Company Law took effect on 1 July 2024; if your company is not registered in mainland China, don't apply the shareholding lines here.
- **Key questions**: Was the initial split based on capital contributed, on the current division of work, or on future contribution? If someone leaves after a year, what happens to their shares — do the articles provide for a mandatory buy-back? Which side of the 34%, 51% and 67% lines are my voting rights on, and have the articles been amended?
- **Preferred plays**: (1) Get past the problem of the first split hardening: before bringing investors in, write down each person's percentage, which line each is on, who makes the call in a deadlock, and what rule governs later adjustments, and have everyone agree; (2) Vesting plus mandatory buy-back: split equity into an immediately vested part and an instalment part, so anyone who leaves early takes away only the vested portion, and put the buyer, price and process in the articles; (3) Control — know which shareholding line you're on, then see what the articles can change: under the default rules, 7 kinds of major matters in a limited liability company need at least two-thirds of the voting rights, so 34% can at least block them, 51% short of 67% still can't decide them alone, and the articles can override a dozen or so default rules.
- **Common traps**: Assuming 51% means you call the shots (under the default rules, short of 67% you still can't decide the 7 kinds of major matters alone); assuming that signing an agreement to act in concert makes your control secure (it lapses at expiry, it may be dissolved once its purpose is achieved, and it does not bind new holders after inheritance or transfer); splitting by capital contributed alone and agreeing nothing else (a year later a co-founder leaves yet still holds a big stake and won't give it back, or is willing to but the price is disputed).
- **Evidence**: Mostly one equity practitioner's experience and individual cases, with no comparison data; the relatively firm parts are those grounded in statutes and the listed-company examples of agreements to act in concert failing, but the statutes are the old version, and the tax rates and concessions are also several years old, so they serve only as reminders.
- **Not in this card**: How large the option pool should be, how long the vesting period, how the exercise price is set; financing terms (anti-dilution, vetoes, valuation-adjustment clauses).

## Ask these first

1. Was the initial split based on capital contributed, on the current division of work, or on future contribution? If the business is still in trial and error, will the biggest contributor six months from now still be the person it is today?
2. If someone leaves after a year, what happens to their shares — do the articles provide for a mandatory buy-back? Who buys back, and at what price?
3. Which side of the 34%, 51% and 67% lines are my voting rights on? Are those lines counted by capital contributed or by voting rights, and have the articles been amended?
4. What does the other side want from taking shares, and does it fit our long-term goals? Which rights am I giving up, and could they later transfer their shares without my consent?
5. Do I want to hold for the long term, or cash out soon? That decides which holding vehicle to choose and how the tax works out.
6. Of the things co-founders "just see differently" right now, which will turn into real disagreements a year from now? Have we agreed in advance who makes the call then, and how we part ways?

## Plays

### 1. Before splitting shares, get past the problem of the first split hardening

Once equity has a visible value (say, an investor has put a valuation on it), adjusting it becomes extremely hard, so sort out the structure before bringing investors in.
1. First run through a checklist of risk signals: an even split; more than 3 founding shareholders; large stakes given to part-timers or one-off resource providers; a minority shareholder who is actually running the business; no exit mechanism; an outside investor holding control; too much unpaid registered capital; a spouse's shares without a separation of money and control; articles that simply use the business-registry template. If 3 or more answers are "yes", the structure needs a deeper test (the source only recommends one and doesn't say how to do it). A few questions in the original list are ones where "yes" actually means you did the right thing (such as having considered an exit mechanism); they are rewritten here as uniform risk statements (judgment). The "3" comes with no stated rationale and is only a rough screen; the standard behind the unpaid-capital question has been affected by the new company law and can't be used as is.
2. With a three-way even split, registration usually requires whole percentages, and who gets the extra 1% decides who stands on the 34% line.
3. The risk of an even split is that when you disagree nobody can break the deadlock, and time is the most expensive cost in a startup; but an even shareholding is not an even vote, since the articles can set their own decision rules (see Play 3; this is a combined judgment, as the source doesn't connect the two).
4. While the business is still in trial and error, the most valuable person may change; if the split is fixed once with no room to adjust, the equity will fall out of step (for a fix, see Play 2).
- Done when: these four things are written down and agreed by everyone — each person's percentage, which line each of you is on, who makes the call in a deadlock, and what rule governs later adjustments.

### 2. Write adjustment and exit into rules: vesting plus mandatory buy-back

At the start nobody knows who will contribute more, yet the split has to be set first. The fix is to specify in advance that anyone who leaves early takes away only the vested portion. This approach comes from overseas venture circles and needs adapting before it works in China.
1. Split each founder's equity into two parts: a portion that vests immediately at founding, and the rest that vests in instalments. Example: 20% vests immediately and the other 80% over 4 years; someone holding 30% who leaves after one year takes away 6% (30% × 20%) plus 6% for the first year, 12% in all, and the remaining 18% is bought back by the others at the original capital price.
2. Vesting periods of 3, 4 and 5 years are all in use, and the longer the trial and error, the longer the period may need to be; vesting can be even each year or step up year by year, and you can add a cliff so that the first tranche only arrives after a full year. What the source says about how many years is "usual abroad" is inconsistent, so don't treat any number as the standard. If you really can't tell right now who contributes more, vest only part of it and leave the rest for the shareholders' meeting to allocate later by negotiation, based on contribution.
3. Write it into the articles, and settle in advance: the triggers (dismissal, removal, resignation, retirement, death); who buys back the unvested portion (all shareholders other than the leaver pro rata, the largest shareholder, or the company through a capital reduction — pick one; under the old company law a company generally could not hold its own shares); the buy-back price (the source lists the original capital contribution, the original contribution plus annualised interest, or the previous year's audited net assets); and the process. If it isn't in the articles, the buy-back may lack a legal basis; the buy-back price is a sensitive spot, and if it isn't fixed in advance, a dispute over price can wreck the whole vesting mechanism.
4. Also agree on two things: when a limited liability company converts to a joint-stock company, the articles have to be redrafted and the vesting arrangement may stop working; and what happens to the unvested portion on an acquisition, merger or split-up.
- Done when: the percentages, the period and the pace are written as numbers; and the buyer, price, process and liability for breach are set out in article provisions approved by the shareholders' meeting.

### 3. Control: know which shareholding line you're on, then see what the articles can change

1. In a limited liability company, these 7 kinds of matters — amending the articles, increasing or reducing registered capital, merger, split-up, dissolution, changing the company form — by default need approval from at least two-thirds of the voting rights. So 34% can at least block them; 51% (often called "absolute control") still cannot decide them alone short of 67%; and 67% is the fullest control. These lines are counted by voting rights; the 51% and 67% judgments both come with "unless the articles provide otherwise"; whether the articles can raise the two-thirds threshold or also lower it the source doesn't make clear, so a lawyer must check.
2. Too small a stake carries not even these two procedural entitlements, calling a meeting and seeking dissolution: with under 10% of voting rights you are not entitled to ask for an extraordinary shareholders' meeting or to ask a court to dissolve the company (the source is a single consultation with no outcome, and doesn't discuss whether a holder under 10% has any other route); but 10% is only eligibility — the court still looks at whether the case falls under an enumerated deadlock ground and whether it "cannot be resolved by other means".
3. The articles of a limited liability company can override a dozen or so default rules: dividends and capital-increase subscriptions not in proportion to capital contributed (this requires the agreement of all shareholders), voting rights not in proportion to capital contributed, consent rights and pre-emption rights on external share transfers, whether shareholder status can be inherited, meeting and voting procedures, how the chair of the board is chosen, and so on. Use this as the agenda when you talk to a lawyer about the articles; don't just adopt the business-registry template.
4. In the early stage use a limited liability company; don't jump straight to registering a joint-stock company with a future listing in mind. A joint-stock company does away with the articles-based autonomy described above, other shareholders have no pre-emption right when shares are transferred, and it's easier to lose control; convert only when you really are ready to open up the shareholding structure to outsiders.
5. Write control provisions into the articles before you bring in outside shareholders or start selling down your own stake: amending the articles takes a shareholders' meeting vote, and an amendment made at the last minute may both lack the votes and be challenged because of its sensitive timing; the main battleground for contests is board seats.

### 4. When you're short of votes: an agreement to act in concert, or a limited partnership vehicle

Both tools can concentrate scattered voting rights in your hands, each with its own weaknesses. The practitioner considers an agreement to act in concert less effective than a limited partnership vehicle, but the vehicle carries a tax cost for anyone who wants to hold long term; the source gives no overall ranking.
1. Agreement to act in concert: several shareholders first agree, outside the shareholders' meeting, on a single outward position and then vote it, with anyone who doesn't comply penalised under the agreement. Example: a founder directly holds a little over twenty percent; after signing, the voting rights they can direct rise to somewhere in the forties — by the lines above, enough to block the 7 kinds of major matters but still not to decide them alone (judgment).
2. First ask about three failure points: what happens at expiry (such agreements generally have a term and lapse when it ends; the source's example states it was not extended); if it was signed for a purpose such as a listing, whether it can be dissolved once that purpose is achieved; and whether the new holder is bound when a signatory dies and the shares are inherited, or when shares change hands on an exchange after listing. The remedy is to write it into the articles to strengthen its effect on third parties, but the source doesn't explain why that works or how far. It is a fallback: use it only when minority shareholders won't give up direct shareholding.
3. Limited partnership holding vehicle: the founder serves as general partner and, with a very small contribution, controls the voting rights in the vehicle (the source's example is controlling over 70% with a 1% contribution), while the others serve as limited partners and only receive the returns.
4. But anyone who wants to hold for the long term should work out the tax first. Exit tax treatment varies by locality (property transfer income at 20%, business income at 5%–35%, or a split by type of partner — all three exist); on dividends during the holding period, individual partners pay 20%, while a corporate holder may be exempt from enterprise income tax on dividends received; and an individual holding indirectly through a partnership also misses out on some of the concessions available for direct shareholding. The figures are several years old, and one of the documents they rely on was later withdrawn.
5. Don't chase tax havens: if a local presumptive tax assessment or fiscal rebate conflicts with national tax law, even a written agreement is void and the tax can be clawed back at any time. The source's conclusion is that this only suits people looking to cash out in the short term who face a huge tax bill, and is not for long-term planning.

### 5. Before bringing in a major shareholder or investor, ask about their goals before the price

When one side lacks money and resources and the other supplies them, a "marriage" can happen fast; but a shareholder relationship is long term and isn't held together by money alone, and if the goals differ, problems come later. The goals needn't be identical — roughly the same, with differences on small points, is enough.
1. Ask what you are giving up: close to 80% of the equity means handing over both the helm and the other side's initiative over any future transfer; that is above the 67% line (judgment).
2. The source raises only two questions — which rights you are giving up, and whether the other side can transfer without your consent — and doesn't say which clauses to use to deal with them; its description of an investor's "several layers of intent" is the practitioner's inference and should be taken only as a prompt.

### 6. Setting aside equity for employees: where the pool sits, which vehicle, whether the price hits the accounts

This mainly concerns employees who plan to sell after a listing; all of the source's examples are listed or about-to-list companies.
1. One way to build a reserved pool: the founder sets up a limited partnership and acts as general partner, with an insider holding the remaining interests on trust for the others; shares from departing partners, or small percentages transferred by various parties, go into it, and are transferred to employees when granted. Nominee holding is used because, under a subscribed-capital system, every share must be subscribed by someone. If you plan a domestic listing, the nominee arrangement must be unwound before filing, and options already granted but not yet in their exercise period generally have to be terminated as well.
2. Choosing a vehicle: with direct registration everyone has voting rights, there are lots of documents to sign during the review period, and every departure means going through an exit procedure; with a holding company, selling after listing is taxed twice, once as corporate income tax and once as personal income tax, 40% combined; a partnership is taxed only once and can also separate money from control. The 20% for partnerships in the source is one locality's treatment; local fiscal rebates are local concessions and shouldn't be counted.
3. A company about to list that gives employees equity at a low price may have it treated as "share-based payment": the difference is booked as an expense, reducing profit, and the employees also owe personal income tax (deferrable until transfer if seven conditions are met and the arrangement is filed). There is no hard standard for fair value; it comes down to the judgment of the securities firm and the accountants, and the further from the filing period, the looser it is. Ask them first: is the grant price below fair value, how is that justified, and can the profits absorb it?

## Common traps

- Assuming 51% means you call the shots: under the default rules, short of 67% you still can't decide the 7 kinds of major matters alone, while 34% can block them.
- Assuming that signing an agreement to act in concert makes your control secure: it lapses at expiry, it may be dissolved once its purpose is achieved, and it does not bind new holders after inheritance or transfer.
- Splitting by capital contributed alone and agreeing nothing else: a year later a co-founder leaves yet still holds a big stake and won't give it back, or is willing to but the price is disputed.

## Two schools of thought

**One school: reduce friction by choosing the right people.** Shareholder relationships are long term; before anyone comes in, check whether their goals are roughly aligned, whether you can trust each other, and whether you move at the same pace, rather than looking only at who brings money or resources.

**The other school: assume friction is there from day one.** The points where co-founders "just see things differently" at the start are often the seeds of a later break; treat them as conflicts that haven't yet flared up and plan for them in advance, rather than relying on a sense of rapport. But this is a discipline of observation, not a testable prediction: it can't prove a partnership will break up, and it can easily mistake differences between complementary roles for conflict.

This pair needn't be an either/or: pick people who move at your pace, and still write down the exit and adjustment rules.

**One school: you can't afford to drag it out, so settle decision rights and adjustment rules early.** Time is the most expensive resource in the startup phase, and putting off decisions for long can sink the company.

**The other school: leave room for correction in internal disagreements; don't treat them as us against them from the start.** Point out mistakes seriously while leaving room to fix them; overly aggressive struggle is wrong; only if the other side persists in and widens the mistake does it become confrontation. It doesn't answer the question of who decides that the other side is persisting in the mistake.

How to choose (judgment): the two govern different stages. Things settled beforehand — ratios, decision rights, vesting, buy-back price — should be settled quickly and fixed in writing, which fills exactly the gap of "who decides": use leaving scenarios and prices written down in advance in place of arguing afterwards about who was right. When a disagreement does arise, take the conciliatory route first, provided both sides still share a goal, are still in the same organisation, and the other side can still correct course; when these premises fail (for instance, one side is already moving assets out), the conciliatory route doesn't apply. On tools: if a minority shareholder is willing to switch to indirect holding and you want to hold for the long term, work out the tax first; if they won't, only then fall back to an agreement to act in concert, written into the articles.

## When this doesn't work

- The company isn't a limited liability company: a joint-stock company can no longer agree those dozen or so rules through its articles, so arrangements that relied on the articles (including vesting) have to be redesigned.
- The shareholding lines are only default rules; once the articles change, the lines move.
- Listed or quoted companies: the shareholding lines, secondary-market stake-building thresholds and regulation all differ; equity crises at unlisted companies are mostly infighting among shareholders, not takeovers by outside rivals.
- You want to hold for the long term and have no plan to cash out: a partnership vehicle carries a tax cost, and tax havens suit even less.
- The source doesn't say when an early-stage company should set up an employee holding vehicle, or whether it's needed when there are very few people; nor does it discuss what the buy-back price means for a founder who joined with labour rather than money and never put in any cash.

## Example

- Three brothers agreed to a third each; registration allows only whole numbers, so one got 34% and the other two 33% each. Two years later, after a falling-out over direction, the two wanted to replace the chair, only to find the articles would have to be amended first, and the 34% holder would not agree, so the motion could never pass. The practitioner adapted this from a lawsuit they handled.
- A content company built around an on-camera personality: at the start, the person driving the promotion held the larger share and the person on camera the smaller one, which made sense at the time; after it took off, the core value shifted to the person on camera, whose equity was still in the other party's hands, and the two couldn't agree on strategy, parting amicably two years later. The practitioner calls this "inevitable", which is hindsight.
- An e-commerce company, to get through a funding winter, let a large institution take nearly 80% of the equity; the institution later sold the stake on to a bigger buyer, the founder had to accept it passively, and was pushed out a few years later. The source doesn't compare against whether a veto or a transfer restriction would have changed the outcome.
- A listed company's controlling shareholder sold down repeatedly, going from a little over half to just over ten percent; a rival accumulated shares on the secondary market and became the largest shareholder; only then did the company amend its articles to set thresholds, which it withdrew after regulators questioned the sensitive timing, and the company came close to delisting. The practitioner says that if the articles had been amended before the selldown, the rival "might" have been deterred — a counterfactual with a "might" in it.

## How solid is the evidence

- Mostly one equity practitioner's experience and individual cases, with no comparison data; the cases are mostly retellings of public information, and the attributions of motive (the other side having several layers of intent, a break-up being "inevitable") are the practitioner's inference, not what the parties said.
- "Three people with a third each, and more than 90% fail" was said offhand by one investor, with no statistical source and no contrasting cases of even splits that succeeded.
- The relatively firm parts are those grounded in statutes (the shareholding lines, the matters the articles can set, the 10% threshold) and the listed-company examples of the three failure points of agreements to act in concert; but the statutes are the old version. The practical points for putting vesting into practice in China are the practitioner's own summary, and the source cites just one court decision upholding a mandatory buy-back in the articles.
- The tax rates, concessions and share-based-payment treatment are several years old; some policies have since been cleaned up and documents withdrawn, so they serve only as reminders and can't be used to work out today's figures.

## One-page checklist

- [ ] Each person's percentage, which side of the 34%, 51% and 67% lines each of us is on, and who makes the call in a deadlock are all written down
- [ ] The vesting percentage and period are written as numbers; mandatory buy-back, the buyer, the price and the process are in the articles
- [ ] The articles are not the registry template: voting rights, dividends, transfer consent rights and meeting rules have all been gone through
- [ ] If using an agreement to act in concert: I've asked a lawyer about the three failure points — expiry, purpose achieved, inheritance and transfer
- [ ] I've asked what the other side wants, which rights I'm giving up, and whether I get a say when they transfer
- [ ] I've decided between long-term holding and a near-term cash-out before choosing a holding vehicle; I'm not treating a tax haven as a long-term plan
- [ ] Before granting employees low-priced equity, I've asked the accountants and the securities firm about the effect on profit and personal income tax
- [ ] Every statute, percentage and tax rate involved above has been checked against current rules by a lawyer and a tax adviser

## Not covered by this card

- How large the option pool should be, how long the vesting period, how the exercise price is set, and how to divide it among levels (the 10% and 2% in the source are only illustrations, and the incentive-ratio rules for listed companies can't be used as early-stage reserve ratios); how to buy back granted equity when an employee leaves
- Other shareholding lines for joint-stock companies, NEEQ-quoted companies and listed companies; other control tools such as proxy voting, preferred shares and dual-class shares
- How the two types of partner in a limited partnership vehicle should have their specific control mechanisms agreed
- How to raise the split with a co-founder, and how to quantify each person's contribution
- Financing terms: anti-dilution, vetoes, valuation-adjustment (performance-bet) clauses; the effect of divorce and inheritance on equity
- What the "deeper test" should actually cover once 3 or more items on the self-check list apply
- The specific provisions, tax rates and share-based-payment treatment under current law
- How to negotiate once co-founders have truly fallen out, and how to go through legal proceedings
