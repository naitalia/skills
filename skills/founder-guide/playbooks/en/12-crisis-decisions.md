# Crisis and Hard Decisions

When a company nears the edge, the hard part is often not a lack of options but telling apart, inside "never give up" and "cut your losses in time", what you are giving up and what you are not. In the story of the founder in the sources who survived, he repeatedly abandoned his original approach while holding on to the company and the team (our reading of the narrative, not his own attribution); in another case, the commentator infers that a company locked in by "stop spending and sales fall" doubled down once more, and within two years its sales fell to about a third of the peak. Most of the sourcing is one survivor's after-the-fact recollection, with no comparison to people who gave up; every case here is anonymised, and outcomes are given only as far as the source's account runs.

## Quick take

- **Key questions**: If the worst really happens, what will I do next, and is there a way to get there without passing through that ending? Am I doubling down because of the prospects, or because the moment I stop, sales drop — and do I have a second road? Are the people opposing me giving an independent judgment, or guessing what I want?
- **Preferred plays**: (1) While there is still cash, build a second road: don't keep tallying the worst outcome; ask "if the company really went under, what would I do?" and "is there a way to do it without going under?", then assign a small team at once without waiting for the biggest customer to fall; precondition (inference): the company has something that can be taken out on its own and that someone would want, otherwise the question may lead nowhere; (2) Bad news, tailored to the audience, checking what they currently believe: for news that affects the equity figures in employees' hands, check before the meeting what they believe the figure is and whether they know how close to danger the company is; to investors, the case's move was to cut once, to a level where no one asks you to cut again (a single sample, not a rule); (3) Who decides — direction and the final call are yours, information comes from the team: on life-or-death matters, consulting employees, the board and outside advisers often gets you nowhere, so ask them for information and data, not for direction.
- **Common traps**: Reading "don't give up" as "keep doubling down"; expanding at the speed of "money is no object" while your customers and your financing ride the same wave of enthusiasm (a reading of the case, with no "go slower" comparison; judgment); trying to pay to suppress a set of damaging reports (the source marks this as rumour only), then having no effective response when the crisis arrives.
- **Evidence**: Almost all of it is one founder's after-the-fact recollection: one company, one person, a surviving outcome, with no outcomes for those who gave up in the sources; the evidence for "don't give up" comes only from asking successful people.
- **Not in this card**: Criteria for whether to keep doubling down, and how to estimate how long cash will last (the sources have no such method); how to wind down when it truly can't continue: orderly closure, liquidation, obligations to creditors and customers.

## Ask these first

1. If the worst really happens, what will I do next? Does the answer include something I truly want to do and can do? Is there a way to get there without passing through that ending?
2. Am I about to give up the company, the team and the mission — or only my original way of doing things? (Judgment)
3. The move I'm refusing to make: is it refused because it's really a bad move, or because I'm afraid of looking incompetent and can't bear to concede?
4. Am I doubling down because of the prospects, or because the moment I stop, sales drop? Do I have a second road?
5. Whose holdings will this news change, and what do they believe the figure is right now? Do they know how close to danger the company is?
6. Are the people opposing me giving an independent judgment, or guessing what I want? When I lean toward the option "everyone agrees on", is it because it's best, or because if it goes wrong I'll be blamed least?

## Plays

### 1. While there is still cash, build a second road

1. Don't keep tallying the worst outcome. The founder found the answer was the same every time and his mood never improved (one person's experience). Ask instead: if the company really went under, what would I do?
2. Then ask: is there a way to do it without going under? List what must be separated from the current business and estimate the time; his technical lead estimated nine months, which proved optimistic.
3. Assign a small team at once (about ten people in the case) and start, without waiting for the biggest customer to fall.
4. Write down any decision you can't think through, in enough detail to explain it to the board; when taking a corner, look at the road, not the wall.
- Precondition (inference): the company has something that can be taken out on its own and that someone would want. If it doesn't, the first question may lead nowhere.

### 2. If you must sell, break it into a process

1. Analyse the industry first, strike out buyers who are in trouble themselves, and narrow the list to the few most likely.
2. Rank buyers by who needs you more, not by who shows interest; for one that hasn't responded, redraw their org chart and look for someone you haven't yet approached who might be able to decide.
3. Host two buyers at the same time so they compete, and set a deadline for the final stage. The founder admits the original deadline was fake and expected it might slip, yet felt having one beat having none; this was one director's conviction, the source doesn't weigh its cost, so judge it yourself before using it.
4. Negotiate structure, not just price: have the buyer take on the debt and cash burn while you keep the intellectual property, the software and the core team.
5. On announcement day, stay at the company and tell everyone, without delaying a minute, whether they are moving to the buyer, staying, or leaving. The founder's lesson: if you don't treat the people leaving fairly, the people staying will never trust you again.

### 3. Bad news: tailor it to the audience and check what they currently believe

1. To investors: if you must cut, the case's move was to cut once, to a level where no one asks you to cut again (a single sample, not a rule). In the case the finance manager's argument was that nobody would believe an optimistic forecast anyway; the company lowered its full-year forecast by about a quarter, cut spending and laid off about 15% of staff at the same time, the underwriters dropped research coverage, the share price fell sharply, and actual results for the year came in only slightly above the new target.
2. To the market: after the biggest customer went bankrupt, the founder disclosed the loss first, even though the share price halved and the financing fell through, because without disclosure there could be no financing.
3. To employees: for news that affects the equity figures in their hands, check what they believe before the meeting. In the case, some managers had inflated the value per share, employees were working out their gains on imagined numbers, got half cut away at the meeting, and — not knowing how close bankruptcy was — reacted with anger; the founder counted this as his own failure. Whether explaining it beforehand would have avoided the anger, the source doesn't say (judgment).
4. For a plan to give up the core business: until the deal was settled the founder let only one colleague among staff and management know (he also explained it to one director), reasoning that once word got out the share price might go to zero and the chance to transfer the business would vanish; on the day the deal was fixed, everyone's fate had to be explained.

### 4. Who decides: direction and the final call are yours; information comes from the team

1. On life-or-death matters, consulting employees, the board and outside advisers often gets you nowhere; ask them for information and data, not for direction.
2. The hardest and most important decisions are often fiercely opposed by your most important supporters; the views of the majority sway your judgment and make an obvious choice hard to call, and going with the crowd draws less blame if it turns out wrong.
3. Gather the information you'll need systematically, all the time: competitors' possible moves, technical feasibility and timing, the company's strengths, financial risk, employee attitudes. The founder relays a case-study comparison claiming that a CEO deciding usually holds less than a tenth of the total information.

### 5. Recognise whether you're in good times or bad, then shift gears

1. Good times: the company has a strong and rising competitive edge in its core field. Bad times: it faces a threat to its survival — not merely ordinary difficulty.
2. In bad times everyone has to execute your decisions around the central mission, other ideas set aside. The founder's contrast: in good times, delegate and tolerate small deviations; in bad times, treat details as life and death and often decide alone. This is description, not a manual, and he draws no line on how far to go; in the same discussion he also asks for enough openness to catch your own mistakes in time, and doesn't say how to combine the two. This bad-times mode applies only to a real threat to survival, not to ordinary trouble.

### 6. Keep a grip on your own mind

1. Under pressure it's easy to slide to one of two extremes: putting everything on yourself (extroverts rage, introverts sink into gloom) or feeling it has nothing to do with you (dodging through excessive optimism, problems left unhandled, the company falling apart). The ideal is to act decisively while staying rational, not enslaved by guilt.
2. Treat the thought "this time it's really over" as a moment nearly every company hits: the founder's partner concluded a company meets it two to five times, and the founder himself at least ten (the sample isn't given).
3. Make friends who have been through something similar, without expecting them to hand you answers; write your thoughts down.

## Common traps

- Reading "don't give up" as "keep doubling down".
- Expanding at the speed of "money is no object" while your customers and your financing ride the same wave of enthusiasm (a reading of the case: the founder blames an external shock, and there is no "go slower" comparison; judgment).
- Trying to pay to suppress a set of damaging reports (the source marks this as rumour only), then having no effective response when the crisis arrives.

## Two schools of thought

**1. Keep going versus cut your losses**

**One school: don't give up, find a way through.** The founder asked successful CEOs for advice and the outstanding ones tended to give the same answer: they had never quit. He himself wanted to quit countless times, and he'd seen people turn to drink or stop trying, none of whom became an outstanding CEO (people he'd seen, not statistics).

**The other school: escalation of commitment — admit it when it's time to admit it.** The biggest cost to a business is not trial and error but a leader who won't concede a mistake: to save face they keep going, or add more, though they know it is wrong, and the cost of saving face can exceed the cost of fixing the problem. There is also a lock-in that isn't about face: the cost of stopping (sales dropping at once) is foreseen, so the only move is to put in another round (the commentator's inference about one case, which has a rival reading — the bid was only for show — that the source cannot settle).

The two are less opposed than they look (our reading, not the source's; judgment): the founder's "don't give up" means the company, the team and the mission, not the original method; the way he survived was precisely by abandoning the old road, selling the core business and cutting his forecast in one go. Admitting error doesn't mean changing direction constantly, though from outside the two are hard to tell apart, and the source doesn't deal with that cost. Where they really collide is in two places: the founder's refusal of one remedial move (a reverse share split) included not wanting to be seen as conceding — exactly the face that escalation of commitment warns about — and this time it worked out, which doesn't endorse the motive; and the evidence for "don't give up" comes only from asking successful people.

How to choose (judgment): the sources contain no rule for "continue or stop", only four tests set side by side. One: is there a second road, something that can be lifted out on its own? If yes, give up the old road and not the company; if not, treat "we must put in one more round" as an alarm, not as a task. Two: is growth being held up by one-off bought attention or outside money, so that it falls when you stop? Three: do your demand side and your funding side ride the same wave, so that both break at once when it recedes? Four: write down why you don't want to give up, and mark how much of it is face.

**2. Consult little and keep secrets versus tell the truth and share the load**

**One school:** discussing life-or-death matters with employees is pointless; in the case the plan to give up the core business was kept from the four hundred or so employees in that business until the deal was settled.

**The other school:** the same founder, elsewhere in his account (only summarized here), says not to carry all the responsibility alone, to find as many people as possible to solve the problem together, and in a staff meeting said bluntly that without stopping the bleeding fast they were finished — and no one flinched; in the part this card draws on, employees' anger at not knowing how close the danger was is also a failure he owns.

How to choose (judgment): keep secrecy to the one plan that would sink the deal, and only until the deal is settled; direction and the final call are yours, but information and responsibility can be shared out; anything touching individuals' futures gets explained that same day. Don't remember only "in a crisis, keep it secret" or "in a crisis, be fully transparent" — each has counterexamples in the sources.

**3. Courage versus conformity as a structural problem**

The founder puts decision-making down to courage, which he says can be trained. Another source holds that conforming isn't weak will: the instrument for testing a group's belief also comes from the group, and from inside it you cannot in principle see that the group is wrong (that is too absolute: where things can be checked independently an individual can see the group's error — the hard part is not being shaken afterwards by evidence pointing the other way). Start with a check that doesn't depend on courage: are the opponents giving an independent judgment, or guessing what I want? In the case, a founder wavering over whether to sell found his board and management leaning toward selling mainly because they could see his indecision; the moment he decided not to sell, they all came round to backing him. The examples of being right against the majority are all CEOs' own recollections; none of the examples collected here is a case of going against the majority and turning out wrong.

## When this doesn't work

- The company has nothing separable that anyone wants: the question of what you would do if it really went under may lead nowhere.
- With only one buyer, "make two buyers compete" has nothing to work on, and the source doesn't say what to do with a single buyer; if the buyers don't need you, ranking by who needs you more has little to grip (judgment).
- Going public in the case was an extreme choice, made when private funding had shut and the public market was still open a crack; it is not evidence that you can go public unprepared, and with no similar exit today it hardly transfers (inference).
- The success of "secrecy plus one person deciding" rested (judgment) on conditions the founder didn't control: a separable asset, a buyer and enough time; what happens when they're missing, the source doesn't say.
- "Cut once and cut deep" assumes credibility is already fragile and the full-year target truly unreachable; if a small adjustment could have held, this case doesn't support it.

## Examples

- An enterprise-services company raised a large sum less than two months after founding, and an investor asked him to imagine how he'd run it if money were no object; within months headcount went from dozens to hundreds and the premises were changed again and again. The industry bubble then burst, most customers turned out to be start-ups from the same wave, quarterly sales reached only a little over a third of forecast, and everyone who might have put in money refused. The founder attributes it to an external shock far beyond expectation and does not judge the pace of expansion to have been wrong; another reading is that demand and funding were riding the same wave. There is no comparison of "what if we'd gone slower".
- The same company, after a rival collapsed, had prepared a second road: carve out its own in-house software, with a small team started at once. Later its biggest customer went bankrupt and funding fell through; the founder let only one colleague among staff and management know about the sale, and seven weeks later sold the core business to a large services firm — the buyer taking on the debt and cash burn, the company keeping the software and core engineers; earlier, some employees had argued against carving out the software with a stack of slides, and he brushed them aside. He called it the best decision he had ever made and said that had employees, investors and customers voted, they would certainly have rejected it (a counterfactual, untested).
- After the core business was sold, the share price fell to a few tenths of a dollar and the exchange gave three months to get back above its minimum listing price. The board thought a reverse split, accepting low-price status, or rallying sponsors were all acceptable; the founder was unwilling to do the reverse split, chose the sponsor route, and through an introduction went to see a veteran investor; within months the price recovered to a few dollars. Years later the investor said he knew nothing about the business and had backed the courage of two people who came in person to ask for help. The success came down to one introduction and one person's judgment, and there is no comparison with the other road.
- A contrast: a county-level small factory became a hit through a one-off heavy advertising push, won the top slot on national television advertising by bidding, and within three years multiplied its sales dozens of times. The commentator reasons that if it didn't renew the slot its sales would drop sharply as its predecessor's had, so it was locked into doubling down; a set of reports then showed it lacked capacity and bought in much of its raw material, sales fell within two years to roughly a third of the peak, and years later its core asset was auctioned off over unpaid debts. The commentator puts the failure down to a consistently strong-man posture and having no effective response in the crisis; another reading is that there was a gap between the story told in its advertising and how it actually operated, and a different PR posture would still have left the crack. It had no second road (judgment).

## How solid is the evidence

- Almost all of it is one founder's after-the-fact recollection: one company, one person, a surviving outcome; the outcomes for those who gave up are not in the sources. Only proportions and orders of magnitude are used here, not exact figures.
- The evidence for "don't give up" comes only from asking successful people, and "outstanding" is itself defined by results; "a vote would have rejected it" is a counterfactual; the examples of being right against the majority are only his own and an anonymous founder's.
- "Cut once and cut deep" has the result of only one path: the cushion was a few percentage points, and that year still depended on an order that nearly slipped away; whether the share-price drop came from the cut or from the industry's collapse can't be separated; the premise (nobody believes an optimistic forecast) is the finance manager's judgment, untested.
- The steps for selling the company were pulled out of a continuous narrative; they are not a list the founder wrote.
- In the small-factory case, the factual layer comes from contemporary public reports, the attribution layer is the commentator's after-the-fact inference, and the commentator, the consultant and the party collecting the fees each speak from their own position.
- The good-times/bad-times contrast is a generalisation from a few famous leaders and the founder himself, with no comparison sample.

## One-page checklist

- [ ] I've written down "what I'll do if the worst happens, and whether I can get there without it", and assigned a team
- [ ] I know which part of the company can be taken out on its own and that someone would want
- [ ] What I'm giving up is a method, not the company, the team and the mission (judgment)
- [ ] For the move I'm unwilling to make: I've written down why and marked how much of it is face
- [ ] Before doubling down: would it drop if I stopped? Do I have a second road? Are demand and funding riding the same wave?
- [ ] For news to employees: I've checked what they believe and whether they know how close the danger is
- [ ] Secrecy limited to one plan and only until the deal is settled; on the day it's settled, everyone's fate is explained
- [ ] For a sale: buyers ranked by who needs me more, two at once, a deadline, structure negotiated
- [ ] Whether my opponents are judging independently or guessing at what I want; whether I'm choosing the majority to be blamed less
- [ ] I have someone who has been through something like this that I can phone, and my thoughts are written down
