# Early Acquisition and Growth

This card puts acquisition, retention and word-of-mouth in one order of work. First pick an activity measure that shows users actually getting value, then see whether the leak is in acquisition or retention. For acquisition, do the economics before choosing channels; for retention, measure correctly before fixing the product and the onboarding; and spreading divides into in-product referral and content-driven word of mouth, whose evidence differs a great deal in strength. Channel and platform names go stale fast, so this card covers only how to screen and how to judge.

## Quick take

- **Key questions**: From not knowing you to experiencing the core value, which step loses the most people, and is the leak in acquisition or in retention? How much does one user bring over their lifetime, what does it cost to get one, and which kind of channel can your business model afford? Does anyone actually like the product? If not, referrals and acquisition will probably buy you only a brief burst of excitement.
- **Preferred plays**:
  - **Pick an activity measure, then draw the funnel**: choose one absolute-number measure that shows users experiencing the core value, and give every step of the journey a number (estimates count).
  - **No ad budget: do the math, then screen channels, then run small tests**: a user's lifetime value has to exceed the cost of acquiring them; test the screened channels in a small way, track cost and user value, then scale up, change tactics or drop.
  - **Users won't stay: measure correctly, then fix the product and onboarding**: measure retention with a key behavior and cohort curves, fix the product and new-user onboarding first, and only last email and push.
- **Common traps**: Defining retention by logins; picking a main channel that doesn't suit the product, having no exit rule for a channel that isn't working, or relying on one channel or one platform's interface; treating "people who use several features retain better" as causal.
- **Evidence**: The metric, funnel, channel and retention frameworks are mainly one practitioner's long experience, with no controlled data, and the cases are single cases or after-the-fact accounts; the six word-of-mouth questions were induced after the fact from things that had already spread, with no comparison against things that hadn't.
- **Not in this card**: Concrete zero-budget cold-start tactics (where the first ten or hundred users come from and how to win them by hand); how to find the key behavior and the activation point.

## Ask these first

1. Are you watching a cumulative count that only goes up, such as total sign-ups, or an active-user number that shows people experiencing the core value?
2. From not knowing you to experiencing the core value, which step in the journey loses the most people? Is the leak in acquisition or in retention?
3. How much does one user bring you over their lifetime, and what does it cost to get one? Which kind of channel can your business model afford?
4. Does anyone actually like the product? If not, referrals and acquisition will probably buy you only a brief burst of excitement.
5. Of what you call word of mouth, how much is trackable online numbers and how much happens where you can't see it?

## Plays

### 1. Pick one activity measure worth watching, then write the journey as a funnel

Total sign-ups is a static number that only grows and carries an element of vanity. Daily, weekly or monthly active-user trend lines are no good for reading retention: they are the net of additions and losses, and new users can cover up the losses.
1. Run the candidate measure through these questions: does it show users experiencing the core value; does it reflect current activity rather than a cumulative total; does improving it mean the whole company is improving (don't miss the other side of supply and demand); can the team easily understand it (an absolute number is advised); is it a leading or lagging indicator; and when it worsens, can you do something about it? Don't aim for perfection first time; the goal is a focus for the team at this stage.
2. Draw the user journey so that each step is an action by the user (judgment), and give each step a metric; if you have no data, estimate and start tracking at the same time. Start by defining just the three most important first-level events, and add more once you can draw a basic funnel.
3. Write total active users as a formula, "new-user part plus existing-user part", and look for the biggest bottleneck, splitting the data by channel, device and new versus old users. Eyeballing is fine as a start, but the step with the lowest conversion is not necessarily the one with the largest effect on the total, so plug in numbers and run what-if comparisons (judgment, worked out from the source's example numbers).
- Done when: you have one absolute-number activity measure and a funnel with a number at every step (estimates count), and you can say which one or two variables to move now and why.

### 2. No ad budget: do the math first, then screen channels, then run small tests

A user's lifetime value has to exceed the cost of acquiring them, or the more you bring in the more you lose (the proponent allows that in particular cases it can be done for a while, but not sustainably); your business model also decides which kinds of channel you can afford.
1. Lifetime value = how many months a user stays × average monthly earnings from them; whether to deduct gross margin is your call. With little early data this is only an estimate, and the more optimistic the estimate the easier it is to fool yourself (judgment). Count acquisition cost fully: for a business-customer product add events, content, and sales salaries and travel, and look separately at the paid channels you control rather than blending in users who arrive on their own.
2. Match against the channels you can afford: a free product's own earnings can't support long-term paid growth, so free channels may be the only option; only when value is high enough to support a sales team does selling make sense. A ratio above 3 is merely what many investors expect of subscription-software companies, not a universal threshold.
3. Five steps for choosing channels: understand the product; understand your users (where they are); list candidates; screen them on five criteria (large volume, free or cheap, trackable, precisely targetable, can be started and stopped at will); then run small tests and track two numbers: the channel with the lowest cost, and the channel whose users stay longest and are worth the most. The aim is low cost bringing high-value users.
4. After testing, decide on each: scale up the good ones, change tactics and retest the weak ones, and drop those that never improve or leave them a very small budget. For most products only one or two channels can really scale, but don't bet on a single channel either.
5. The five criteria are listed side by side, unranked, in the source; a team with a very small budget may do better to look first at trackability and the ability to stop at will, with large volume mattering only after validation (judgment). Use the funnel to see where it leaks before opening new channels.
- With zero budget, the one concrete line in this channel-selection process: sales-type channels start with people presenting and demonstrating one by one, and once the best way is found it becomes a repeatable process.
- Done when: every tested channel has two numbers, cost and user value (in the short run, activation rate and paying rate can stand in), and a verdict: scale up, change tactics or drop.

### 3. Getting people to spread it: in-product referral and content-driven word of mouth are two layers

In-product referral is cheap, brings good-quality users and converts well; in a survey of thirty-odd growth experts by a well-known incubator, over seventy percent named it among the most important early channels (a subjective ranking, small sample).
1. Put the invitation at a shareable moment (a new user has just signed up or just finished a task), not buried in a static menu; then split the flow into steps to find the bottleneck: share of users who see the entry point, invitation-page conversion, invitations sent per person, invitees who open it, and those who complete sign-up.
2. Don't make "every existing user brings at least one new user" your target: that almost never happens, or lasts only a short while; below that number, referral is still very good value.
3. For the content layer, check what you want people to talk about with six questions: does talking about it make people look smarter, richer or more stylish; what setting and what cue would remind people of it; is the emotion it triggers one that encourages sharing or one that kills it; can others see it being used; does it save people time or money; is there a story people will retell? The six need not all be present; this is a checklist, not a recipe.
4. Ask whether the content is worth passing on before looking for people to pass it: a well-known person's appeal isn't enough to make something spread, though that doesn't mean key people are useless, and at cold start getting the right person to show it to the first people may still be worth something (judgment). Don't expect everyone to pass it to several more people; chains are short, and the job is to cut the loss at each step (judgment).
- Done when: every step of the referral flow has a number and you know where it sticks; you've been through all six questions and know which are weakest.

### 4. Users won't stay: measure correctly, then fix the product and onboarding, and only last email and push

Early on, losses are hidden by new arrivals; money can buy new users but not retention, because retention rests on the product being valuable to people.
1. Define "stayed" by a key behavior rather than a login (for a product people mostly return to in order to play, a login may be enough); time it by the product's natural usage cycle: find users who performed the key behavior at least twice, see how far apart most of them were, and then choose daily, weekly or monthly.
2. Draw a cohort curve: group users who first performed the key behavior in the same period, and for each period compute the share still performing it. A good curve falls and then flattens; a curve that keeps dropping suggests the product may not yet fit the market. After you improve onboarding, newer cohorts should be flatter or higher.
3. The order of fixes: product improvements and new-user onboarding first, then email and push; for a product that hasn't been optimized the former usually pays far more than the latter (the proponent's claim; no comparison data across tactics), and a barrage of messages also tires users out.
4. Diagnose why users aren't performing the key behavior: not enough motivation, not enough ability (too much effort, a confusing interface), or no trigger. Then sort the retained users by engagement level (passive, core, super) and find which level most are stuck at.
- Done when: you have a cohort curve built on the key behavior, you know whether it flattens and at what level, and you know which level most retained users are stuck at.

## Common traps

- Defining retention by logins: a small group logs in diligently but uses the product as a private notebook, and most leave when the trial ends.
- Picking a main channel that doesn't suit the product, having no exit rule for a channel that isn't working, or relying on one channel or on one platform's open sharing interface: when the platform changes its rules, the source dries up.
- Treating "people who use several features retain better" as causal: heavy users may simply have been more committed to begin with.
- Doing many things at once hoping one lands, or launching without basic monitoring.

## Two schools of thought

**Group one: should trackable channels come first, or does word of mouth mostly happen where you can't see it?**

**One school: channels should be trackable.** When choosing channels, favor trackable ones, and quantify referral with a funnel; only then can you work out cost and retention and decide what to scale up or drop.

**The other school: don't mistake online noise for word of mouth.** This school says most word of mouth happens offline and under a tenth online (no source given); online comments are overrated because they are visible. Putting trackability first naturally favors channels that can be quantified online.

How to choose (judgment): turn trackable channels into funnels and small tests; for untrackable ones, ask at sign-up or checkout "how did you hear about us?" and estimate an order of magnitude. Where your target group talks face to face and is swayed by people they know, offline carries more weight; in heavily online communities the online share may be far higher, so measure it in your own audience.

**Group two: does growth strategy matter more, or building something real first?**

**One school: acquiring users is the other half that decides life or death.** A product's success depends less and less on a fresh feature and more and more on growth strategy.

**The other school: build substance first, then build the name.** Overdoing the name is a trap: the moment consumers suspect that a big slice of the price is advertising, the product is finished; winning through the name works mainly for consumer goods whose effect buyers can't judge for themselves. The social conditions it cites, such as herd-like consumers and authoritative media, are out of date, while the product conditions still hold (judgment).

How to choose (judgment): the first school itself says money can't buy retention, so the two converge on "have a product worth staying for". For a product whose effect users can check for themselves and that is used repeatedly, retention decides; for consumer goods whose effect is hard to judge, building the name really can work, but it must not be rushed. Building the name here means a reputation forced through paid media, while content-driven word of mouth is organic spread; don't mix them up (judgment).

## When this doesn't work

- Very early, with few users: each cohort has only a few dozen people so curves jitter, and the growth model is more a list of hypotheses to verify; at that point it may be steadier to check whether each new user completed the key behavior (judgment).
- Rarely used or one-off products (buying a home, renovation and the like): the value isn't in repeat use, so look at repeat purchase and referral rather than a retention curve (judgment); and if the key behavior is wrong, the whole curve measures the wrong thing.
- Viral spread has product preconditions: free, no barrier to entry, value delivered quickly, needed by everyone, better the more people use it; a product that takes a while to show a return can hardly use it as its main channel.
- The channel-selection process is designed around "spend to acquire, then work out the cost"; with zero budget the first users usually come from hand-to-hand effort (judgment), and platform rules and interfaces change.

## Examples

- A social app grew explosively on a platform's open posting feature, reaching monthly actives in the tens of millions, and lost almost half of them within two months of a funding round. Its founder said afterwards that new users arrived, found no value and left quickly; the immediate trigger was the platform shutting down the posting feature the app depended on. Lesson: the evidence is mainly the founder's after-the-fact account, and two explanations coexist, a channel cut off and new users finding no value.
- A small-appliance company with a good but unknown product: its marketing director spent a few tens of dollars on props and had the owner, in a lab coat, grind hard objects to powder in the machine on camera. The proponent says the video got millions of views in its first week and sales rose several-fold in two years. Lesson: an uncontrolled success story with no baseline for the sales figure, and the hook was the product's performance itself; whether a product without an obvious demonstration can copy it, the case doesn't answer.
- A television brand barely a year old paid a few million for exclusive sponsorship of a nationally buzzed event. For the moment, awareness, purchase intent and orders all rose, and it was named one of the year's top marketing campaigns; a year later it left the market. The channels, product and price war in between are not explained, so the failure can't be pinned on this campaign; and like the previous example this is "figures for the moment plus the proponent's attribution", so don't use one to refute the other.

## How solid is the evidence

- The six metric questions, the growth model, the five-step channel selection and the four-step cohort curve are mainly one practitioner's long experience, with no controlled data; the cases are single cases or after-the-fact accounts. In one team's model, improving new-user onboarding yielded several times the daily-active growth of one-off acquisition after half a year, but that is model output, not an experiment; the retention compounding example also assumes constant additions and a constant loss rate and shows only direction.
- Don't treat unsourced numbers as benchmarks: under a tenth of word of mouth being online, twenty to fifty percent of purchase decisions being influenced by word of mouth, forty percent monthly retention for new users of one social product.
- "Without retention, you die" is a strongly rhetorical conclusion; the evidence is just two cases and two worked examples, and it sits alongside "acquiring users is the other half", so don't quote either line alone.
- For the word-of-mouth layer, no test data is given for any of its principles, and the six questions were induced after the fact from things that had already spread, with no comparison against things that hadn't. Later research: an analysis by the same proponent supports "some emotions encourage sharing and some kill it" (not independent); an independent team's study of online diffusion supports "chains are short"; and a computer simulation supports "no need to rely on a few key spreaders", though it is a simulation and does not test "the content itself decides spread".
