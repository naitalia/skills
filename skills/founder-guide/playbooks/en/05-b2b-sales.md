# Selling Big Deals to Businesses

A big deal is not a small deal scaled up: the cycle is long, many people are involved, the person who really decides is often absent, and a bad purchase gets exposed in front of colleagues. So the outcome rides mainly on the discovery work up front and on the one concrete action you win at each meeting, not on a hard close at the end. "Let us think it over" may be a symptom (judgment): first see what action they agreed to, then look upstream at whether the problem was ever made big enough. To get from early adopters into the mainstream, pick one small, concrete segment and go all in on it, rather than working several at once.

## Quick take

- **Key questions**: Is this a big deal (several meetings and several people, the real decider often absent, a bad purchase visible to colleagues)? At the end of the last meeting, what specific action did the customer agree to take, or were you left with only "very interested" and "we'll be in touch"? Did the customer merely admit a problem, or have they started talking about action and making concrete demands of the solution?
- **Preferred plays**:
  - **Discovery: ask before you tell**: use the four question types, background, difficulty, impact and value (a guideline, not a script), to turn the problem the customer admits into an explicit requirement in their own words; don't recite product highlights before that, and have your contact say the value in their own words.
  - **Win one concrete action at every meeting**: before a visit, write the goal as one thing the customer will do; when you hear "let us think it over", see whether an action or date they agreed to follows, and if not, log it as a stall (inference; they may genuinely need internal process time).
  - **After the early adopters, pick one small, concrete segment**: screen with the four veto questions, back exactly one segment and concentrate your resources on it.
- **Common traps**: Treating the number of pain points surfaced as lead quality, or "very interested" as a successful meeting; using assumptive, either-or or ultimatum-style closing lines on savvy professional buyers (the bigger the decision, the more people resist, but not pushing at all doesn't work either); asking a long string of background questions while asking too few impact questions.
- **Evidence**: The split between small and big deals, the four question types and the claim that explicit requirements predict a sale all come from the originating team's observations around the 1980s, the last resting mainly on one study of over a thousand large deals with no independent replication; the segment-selection half is a consultancy's long experience, and "pick only one, never run two" is the proponent's assertion.
- **Not in this card**: What exactly to say after hearing "let us think it over" and how to rescue a stalled deal; how to get the first big-customer meetings when starting from zero (leads, cold start, reaching the person who decides).

## Ask these first

1. Is this a big deal? Does it need several meetings and several people, is the real decider often absent, would a bad purchase be visible to colleagues? The more of these are true, the less you can reuse small-deal scripts.
2. At the end of the last meeting, what specific action did the customer agree to take? Or were you left with only "very interested" and "we'll be in touch"?
3. Did the customer merely admit a problem, or have they started talking about action and making concrete demands of the solution?
4. Is the problem urgent enough to outweigh the cost to them? The cost is not just money; it includes the personal risk of buying wrong.
5. When I'm not in the room, who will relay this to the boss or to finance? Can they explain its value?
6. When choosing a segment to attack: is there a single, reachable decision-maker with the budget to pay for the complete offering? Could the customer live with this problem for another year?

## Plays

### 1. Discovery: turn the problems customers admit into requirements they state themselves

Opening routines, hard closes and point-by-point objection handling that work on small deals have limited effect on big ones, because the buyer's situation has changed. In big deals, the number of pain points you draw out has almost no link to the outcome; what matters is whether those pains later become explicit requirements in the customer's own words. The sources give no dollar line between small and big deals, so you have to judge which side your product falls on.
1. When reviewing a lost deal, first check whether discovery drew out explicit requirements, and only then blame the closing. Salespeople written off as poor closers were often, in fact, weak earlier in the process.
2. Before a visit, write down three problems the customer is likely to have (each one something you can solve). Under each, write difficulty questions; then follow each difficulty to the more serious consequences it leads to, and turn those into impact questions. Unless you write them in advance, impact questions are hard to come up with in the room.
3. In the room, move through four kinds of questions: background, difficulty, impact, value. Keep background questions few and purposeful; difficulty questions find dissatisfaction; impact questions grow the consequences layer by layer; value questions get the customer to state the benefit of solving it. This is a guideline, not a script; if the customer opens with an explicit requirement, go straight to value questions.
4. Impact questions should enlarge consequences the customer can actually name, not ones you assume on their behalf (in the source's worked example every figure was supplied by the customer; this rule is an addition to the example). They work especially well on the person who holds the budget; with end users, a few simple difficulty questions usually already have a positive effect. Too many in a row discourage people; then switch to value questions.
5. Don't ask value questions too early, and don't ask them about needs you can't meet; in that case, move on to discussing solutions.
- Done when: the customer starts talking about action and making specific demands of the solution, rather than just nodding that the problem is real. The action-talk signal comes from a single salesperson's interview and may be mere politeness.

### 2. Ask before you tell, and let your contact speak for you when you're not there

In big deals the outcome depends more on how the participants sell to one another, and the main part probably happens while you're absent; a contact relaying their own needs is more reliable than one relaying your product.
1. Before explicit requirements are on the table, don't recite your product's highlights. Statements aimed at a requirement the customer has already voiced track closely with winning; generic benefits help little in big deals and fade as the meetings go on. For a new product, the same applies: first work out which customers' which problems it solves.
2. If the customer asks for a product introduction right away, talk beforehand to the key person among the attendees, so part of the introduction speaks to their needs. When customers genuinely want feature detail (technical staff on complex, high-tech products), give it.
3. Use value questions so your contact says, in their own words, which part helps them most; don't present the solution as covering everything, which invites the customer to list everything that needs solving.
4. When they say they'll take something back to finance or the boss, ask what it does for those people. In the worked example the buyer then gave a more important reason and agreed to try to arrange a quarter-hour with the general manager.

### 3. Win one concrete action at every meeting; when you hear "let us think it over", look at the action

The research sorted meeting outcomes into four: a sale; a step forward (agreeing to a demo, letting you meet someone more senior, a trial or test, accepting part of a budget they had refused); a stall (the deal is still alive but the customer agreed to no concrete action, often ending with "we'll be in touch"); and a clear refusal. A stall counts as not successful: judge by what the customer does, not what they say.
1. Before a visit, write the goal as one thing the customer will do: come to your site to discuss, set up a meeting with their boss. "Learn their needs" and "build rapport" can come along, but if they are your only goals the result is usually a stall.
2. Near the end, in order: ask whether there is anything left you haven't explained; gather the hours of discussion into the benefits they get; then propose the next step in the tone of a suggestion, not a request to sign.
3. The next step you propose must meet two tests: it really moves the deal forward, and it is the most the customer can realistically give, no more than they can bear.
4. When the customer says "let us think it over", see whether it is followed by an action or a date they agreed to. The sources never classify that sentence word for word; this follows from the test above: if nothing follows, log it as a stall even if the mood was warm. They may genuinely need internal process time, so go by the action, not the tone.
5. If you proposed a specific next step and they refused (won't meet again, won't let you see someone more senior), it is close to a no.
6. If "let us think it over" becomes the norm, look upstream (judgment): on one side of the scale is urgency, on the other is cost; when it hasn't tipped, explaining more product detail rarely helps and pushing harder may make it worse. Mapping that phrase onto an imbalance is an inference; the sources don't answer it directly.
- Done when: after every meeting you can write the sentence "the customer agreed to ___"; if you can't, record it as a stall.

### 4. After the early adopters: pick one small, concrete segment

Early adopters and mainstream customers have very different psychological profiles, so extrapolating from that experience calls for great care; and you can't break into two places at once, so concentrate your resources on one segment and aim to lead it.
1. Don't take comfort in "the market is this big and we only need a few points of it". In a new segment you have no experience and no data, and size forecasts rest on layers of arbitrary assumptions. Admit the lack of data and calibrate your intuition with vivid pictures of specific customers.
2. Gather people who know the front line and write a one-page scenario for each current customer and each interesting prospect: who the user, the technical gatekeeper and the budget-holder are; the user's stuck day (situation, desired result, current workaround, what goes wrong, the economic consequence); and the day after adopting your solution (the new way, the cost saved or gain made). When the scenarios start repeating, roughly somewhere between twenty and fifty, merge them into eight to ten options.
3. First screen, four veto questions: is there a single, reachable buyer able to pay for the complete offering; are the consequences of leaving the problem unsolved big enough that it can't wait another year (mainstream pragmatic customers will tolerate a problem they can live with for a year, and your salespeople will be invited back for demo after demo without coming back with an order); can you plus your partners ship the complete offering within about a quarter; has a competitor already taken the ground (if so, first see whether you can go around them). A very low score on any one is almost always a veto; don't just add up totals.
4. Second screen, the rest: partners, distribution, pricing (based on the total price of the complete offering, leaving partners something to earn), positioning (not being credible at first is normal; a real commitment to fill the gaps can overcome the resistance quickly), and whether winning this segment opens adjacent ones. Low scores here can usually be fixed with investment and time, which are exactly what you're short of.
5. Size the segment to your own weight: to be treated as the standard by mainstream customers you need to win at least half of the segment's new orders over the next year. If it is too big, cut it along word-of-mouth boundaries; if too small, merge it into a genuine larger segment; if you can't find a genuine larger segment, you should probably go back and choose another. The source's worked numbers are hypothetical; using them only as an order-of-magnitude check is my judgment (the source doesn't reconcile this with its warning against leaning on size forecasts).
6. To decide: a committee as small as possible but including everyone who could veto the outcome scores privately first, then discusses disagreements in the open; the first screen usually removes about two-thirds. Three outcomes: if everyone agrees, go; if you can't decide, have one person line up the candidates by which adjacent segment each would unlock and attack the lead one first; if none survives, don't force a crossing and don't push for growth, keep taking early-adopter projects, hold burn as low as possible and keep looking.
7. Once chosen, commit fully; it needn't be the best choice, but you must win the one you picked. Commission research early to test the premises behind the winning scenario, but don't wait for it before moving.
- Done when: the team backs exactly one segment, and it passed the four veto questions.

## Common traps

- Treating the number of pain points surfaced as lead quality, or treating "very interested" as a successful meeting: the proponent saw customers use polite approval to see a salesperson out more than once.
- Using assumptive, either-or or ultimatum-style closing lines on savvy professional buyers: the bigger the decision, the more people resist pressure, and it may even lower satisfaction after the purchase. But not pushing at all doesn't work either (in small-deal data, never pushing had a lower success rate); what has to change is how you push.
- Asking a long string of background questions (one meeting had thirty-five background questions and none of the other kinds, and the buyer showed them out) while asking too few impact questions (in the studies, one question in twenty).

## Two schools of thought

**One school: use questions to make the customer's small problem big.** When a customer admits a problem but feels they can cope, follow the consequences layer by layer until they themselves feel it is worth acting on.
**The other school: go straight for segments where the reason to buy is already strong.** Mainstream customers will put up with a problem they can live with for another year.
How to choose (judgment; the sources don't reconcile them): they work at different levels. The second decides which segment to attack; the first decides how, inside one deal, to help the customer see the cost. Screen segments with the second; if the problem is small to begin with, there is little room to blow it up by questioning.

**One school: agreeing to attend a demo counts as a step forward.**
**The other school: when the reason to buy isn't compelling, mainstream customers will invite you to demo again and again without ever ordering.**
How to choose (judgment): every step should bring you closer to the person who really decides; repeated demos to the same people, with no more senior attendee, trial or budget following, count as stall after stall.

## When this doesn't work

- Small deals (settled in one call or by self-service): conventional scripts still work; with cheap items, unsophisticated customers and no need for a lasting relationship, hard closing can be very effective; in service sales the usual problem is asking too little.
- When the price is very low, a customer admitting the problem is enough to buy.
- If your contact has no influence, coaching them to sell internally pays off little (judgment); if nobody on the team has met customers in the target segment, a one-page scenario is just imagination (judgment).
- Consumer markets: user, technical gatekeeper and payer tend to merge into one or two roles, and the source says almost all successful crossings have happened in business markets.
- The samples come from business-equipment sales around the 1980s; for self-serve trials and subscriptions you must work out for yourself what counts as a step forward.

## Examples

- A junior salesperson met a telecom customer who agreed that peak-hour capacity was short and data quality unsatisfactory; he judged the meeting a success. Two weeks later the customer was talking to a competitor, who won the deal a few months on. A top-five performer at the same company said what made him feel a deal was alive was the customer starting to talk about what they would do next year. These are two interviews, not statistics.
- A senior salesperson wanted the customer's chief engineer to visit the plant to discuss feasibility. The engineer didn't come, but in the meeting he learned the customer had just won a chance to build a new plant, and on the spot asked the contact to set up a meeting with a relevant engineer, and got a date. His boss called him a strong closer; the proponent went on several visits and never saw him use a pressure line. The proponent's reading is that he succeeded through how he set the goal before the visit; this is a single case.

## How solid is the evidence

- The split between small and big deals, the order of the four question types, and the claim that explicit requirements predict a sale while admitted problems do not all come from the originating team's observations around the 1980s; the last rests mainly on one study of over a thousand large deals, with no independent replication. The lead proponent himself says the four question types merely describe more precisely what experienced salespeople already do, and says he doesn't quite understand why generic benefits fade over the course of a sale. That discovery matters most rests on a written conclusion only, with no quantitative charts seen, and how much each stage weighs varies by deal.
- The value equation (urgency against cost) is a qualitative framework conveyed through diagrams and examples, with no way to measure it; the example dialogue is a small illustration by the proponent, not a recorded measurement.
- Hard closes failing: several studies, each with flaws even the proponent admits; the "expensive goods" that were directly tested averaged only about a hundred dollars, and extending that to thousands or tens of thousands is inference. The direction is credible; the strength is doubtful.
- The four meeting outcomes are a scoring rule used in the research, not a verdict on customers' psychology; an action the customer agrees to may also be mere courtesy, and nobody checked that layer. The drug company's test group selling about half as much again in year one was the proponent's own experiment with an undisclosed design; treat it as a direction only.
- The closing steps and writing the goal as one customer action are the proponent's qualitative summary of what effective salespeople tend to do, with no per-step effect data.
- The segment-selection half is the proponent's consultancy experience: "pick only one, never run two" is his assertion; scoring is subjective, the last three items come with no scoring basis, and whether a win opens adjacent segments is a judgment about the future; the scenario material contains fiction and bias, and there is no data testing the checklist's predictive power; winning at least half the new orders is an assertion from experience; "intuition beats analysis" rests only on analogy and consulting experience, so treat it as a practice for when data is missing, not a general rule; "commit now, don't wait for research" is argued on the grounds that delay favours incumbents, with no data, and sits in tension with the common sense of validating small before scaling.

## One-page checklist

- [ ] I've judged whether this is a big deal or a small one
- [ ] Before each visit, the goal is written as one action the customer will take
- [ ] Before the meeting I wrote three likely problems, plus difficulty and impact questions
- [ ] My review asks three things: what action did they agree to, was it a problem or an action they spoke of, and was the problem made big?
- [ ] My contact can state the value in their own words, and I haven't finished the product tour before drawing out explicit requirements
- [ ] Segment: all four veto questions passed, only one segment, sized to my own weight
