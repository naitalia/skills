# Managing the Team

Once a team grows, the most common mistake is treating "busy" as "productive", managing everyone the same way, and leaving meetings and decisions to improvisation. The core judgment: first work out how much organisational output each of your activities actually buys, then fix a few small routines (delegate plus spot-check, one-on-ones, discipline for decision meetings, a discuss–decide–commit sequence) that set the rhythm for information and decisions, and let the tightness of your grip follow how well each person knows the specific task at hand. The numbers and examples below come almost entirely from one practitioner's experience inside one large company.

## Quick take

- **Key questions**: Where did my time go this week — which one or two activities reach many people at once, keep working long after a single sentence, or give a group the information they need, and which ones subtract value? For the decision in front of me, can I answer all six: what is being decided, by when, who decides, whom to consult first, who can veto it, and who must be told afterwards? How well does this person know this specific task, and has their task or environment changed recently?
- **Preferred plays**: (1) Change the yardstick: don't ask "am I busy?", list this week's main activities and ask what each buys in organisational output; out of a pile of things that all look important, choose one or two, three at most, and give them everything, and find the negative-multiplier activities (delaying decisions, a low mood spreading, interference from above, walking into a meeting unprepared); (2) Deciding: welcome every argument, make the decision clear rather than blurring it, and secure commitment — not everyone has to be convinced — asking the six things above before you act; (3) Adjust your style to how well they know this task: low familiarity, clear detailed instructions; rising, more communication and emotional support; higher, just confirm their effort points the way the department needs; when circumstances change, go back to more specific direction.
- **Common traps**: Failing at both ends — vanishing after you hand off, or meddling until you've designed their plan for them; the senior person speaks first and those who know the subject stay quiet, or peers probe the room and speak only once a consensus has formed; taking someone's personal ability for their familiarity with a new task.
- **Evidence**: Almost all of it is one practitioner's long experience in one large company, plus a handful of examples, with no samples or controls; every number is a rule of thumb, and the "multiplier" has no measurement method and works better as a way of ranking than as a formula.
- **Not in this card**: How to get information when the team is too big to see below you — short informal conversations, walking around, cross-checking several sources; how to judge a person's familiarity level in practice.

## Ask these first

1. Where did my time go this week? Which one or two activities reach many people at once, keep working long after a single sentence, or give a group the information they need? Which ones subtract value: putting off decisions, spreading a low mood through the team, finishing a subordinate's plan for them, walking into a meeting unprepared?
2. How many people report to me directly? How much time can I give each per week, and does it add up to something I can afford?
3. For what I've handed off: is the scope clear? Do we share the same information base and way of working? How will I spot-check?
4. The last time I got a true picture of what's happening below me, was it through regular conversations, or after something broke?
5. For the decision in front of me: can I answer all six — what is being decided, by when, who decides, whom to consult first, who can veto it, and who must be told afterwards?
6. How well does this person know this specific task? Has their task or environment changed recently?
7. In the meeting, am I steering — or am I doing most of the talking, with everyone waiting to read my face?

## Plays

### 1. Change the yardstick: how much organisational output does each activity buy?

A manager produces nothing alone; the output is that of the whole organisation under their control and influence. Judging, instructing, training and coordinating are only activities, and each is worth what it brings in output (called its "multiplier" below).
1. List this week's main activities. Don't ask "am I busy?" — ask what each one buys in organisational output.
2. High multipliers come in roughly three kinds: reaching many people at once; a simple act or a few words with long-lasting effect; supplying knowledge or information that shapes a group's work.
3. Find the negative ones: delaying decisions (often the same as making a wrong one), letting a low mood spread, interference from above, walking into a meeting unprepared.
4. Timing counts too: planning ahead is worth more than repairing afterwards; when a promising person is about to leave, the later you talk to them, the lower your odds of keeping them.
5. Out of a pile of things that all look important, choose one or two, three at most, and give them everything. Another source adds that a complex situation often has one key problem which, once tackled, loosens the others, and that it changes with circumstances so you must re-judge regularly; his rule for spotting it lacks a general test you can apply in advance, so treat it as a hypothesis and adjust as you go.
- Done when: you can name the one or two things you're giving everything to this phase, and one negative-multiplier activity you have already stopped.

### 2. Delegate, but don't disappear

After you delegate, you still answer for the outcome. Supervising is not meddling, and both disappearing and meddling reduce output.
1. First confirm that you and the other person share an information base and a way of working you both accept. If not, tell them the goals, priorities and how you work; patching the gap with layers of rules usually drags efficiency down.
2. Spell out the scope. Don't leave them guessing whether they've been given authority, or how far it goes.
3. Decide how you'll supervise before you hand it over: when you'll look, at what, how often. Check when the work is at its cheapest to change — for example, a draft. Sample at irregular intervals, and sample different people differently.
4. Lower the checking frequency as they get more familiar with the task; give yourself a budget for how often you dive into details, because checking every detail costs too much.
5. For delegated decisions, supervise how they think: have them work out the details before proposing, ask a few key questions, and if the answers are convincing, let it through.
6. Before each check, ask yourself: am I checking, or have I already designed their detailed plan for them?

### 3. Do the headcount arithmetic, then use one-on-ones to pull information up

1. The method travels better than the number: decide how much time each direct report needs from you each week, then divide by the time you actually have. The practitioner's rule of thumb is 6 to 8 reports for a manager whose main job is people; 3 to 4 is too few, 10 too many — assuming about half a day per person per week.
2. Give every direct report their own regular one-on-one. Bumping into each other all day and ad-hoc task meetings do not substitute.
3. Set the frequency by how well they know this particular task and how fast it changes: a new project might need weekly, someone fully on top of it might need monthly. Allow at least an hour; shorter, and people may only raise the easy problems.
4. It's their meeting: they write the agenda and run it, and you get a copy. Start with the metrics that look wrong, then cover people, hiring and planning since last time, and ask them to tell you as soon as they sense something might go wrong.
5. You are the facilitator: when they finish, ask one more question. Both of you take notes; when they write down your suggestion on the spot, that is a commitment, and next time you follow up against it.
6. Park important-but-not-urgent items until next time; don't leave the personal topics to the last few minutes; fix the next date before you part.
7. In a multi-layer or dispersed organisation, one-on-ones only reach your direct reports, so add a written channel: a periodic, length-limited report written by each leader personally, about problems rather than achievements. Its weakness is that everything rests on "they really wrote it themselves", which can't be verified.

### 4. Meetings: diagnose first, then cut

Meetings are the medium for passing on information and making decisions, and they can't be avoided; what you can do is pick the most effective medium for each job.
1. Sort them into two kinds: routine meetings (made cheaper by a fixed rhythm, provided other leaders also schedule around it) and reactive meetings (called ad hoc to solve a specific problem, usually producing a decision). If reactive meetings eat more than a quarter of your time, the practitioner says the organisation has a problem; that line is a rule of thumb with no stated basis.
2. Before calling a decision meeting, ask what decision you need from it and whether there's another way; if you can't say, don't hold it.
3. Do the sum: cost per hour × people × duration, and confirm it's worth the price (use your own team's rate today). Invitees should ask themselves whether they need to be there; cancel unnecessary meetings early in favour of a one-on-one, a call or a note.
4. A decision meeting with more than six or seven people gets a bit hard to move; eight is the ceiling (the practitioner's rule of thumb). No spectators who only watch, and no tolerance for lateness.
5. Circulate an agenda stating the purpose and what each person is to cover; send minutes quickly afterwards: what, who, by when.

### 5. Deciding: open discussion, a clear decision, commitment whether or not people agree

1. Discussion: welcome every argument — the more disagreement, the better. Watch for people waiting for the boss or the majority to speak before they do.
2. Decision: make it clear. Don't blur it to avoid friction; vagueness only angers those who are unhappy.
3. Commitment: not everyone has to be convinced, but they must accept it and carry it out wholeheartedly; your job is to secure everyone's commitment to support the decision.
4. Once the discussion has gathered enough relevant information, move on to deciding — don't let the decision be born premature, and don't drag it out. Decide even without consensus.
5. Before acting, ask six things: what is being decided, the deadline, who decides, whom to consult first, who can veto, and who must be told afterwards. The practitioner can think of no better guard than asking these six against a hard-won consensus being overturned at the last minute; (judgment) the "who can veto" question is the one that most directly puts that risk on the table.
6. If you really must overturn a consensus, give your reasons on the spot and leave time for people to react; don't say your piece and walk away.
7. People of equal rank who meet tend to go in circles (afraid of dissent, afraid of looking foolish, probing for the room's view first). Have someone slightly senior who can steer: normally he gives people confidence and pulls the talk back on topic without doing most of the talking; only once discussion is enough or the deadline closes in does he use his authority, and using it too early can go badly. With no formal chair, the person with the most at stake steps up, otherwise the most senior.
8. Push decisions down to whoever is closest to the problem and understands it best — but note that in the practitioner's own example the people below researched and proposed, and he approved after asking about sources and reasoning; final say was not handed down.

### 6. Adjust your style to how well they know this task

1. Separate "this person" from "this person on this task": a capable person who changes tasks can look like a novice again, and when a task grows more complex or changes abruptly, familiarity drops.
2. Low familiarity: give clear, detailed instructions (what, by when, how to start). Rising: shift toward communication and emotional support. Higher: just confirm their effort points the way the department needs. At every stage, keep a measured eye on the work.
3. When circumstances change (a new task, a crisis), go back to more specific direction instead of forcing yourself to look hands-off; choose a style by familiarity, not by wanting to play good cop or bad cop or by personal taste.
4. Check your self-image: in the practitioner's informal survey, about nine in ten bosses thought they communicated and delegated better than their reports thought they did, and your "suggestions" are often heard as orders. Go and ask.
5. (Judgment) The frequency of one-on-ones, the frequency of spot-checks and the tightness of your style all hang on the same variable: how well they know this task. You can use the one-on-one as your way in to judge it.

## Common traps

- Failing at both ends — vanishing after you hand off, or meddling until you've designed their plan for them; leaving a newcomer to learn by making mistakes when the tuition is really paid by the customer, because the manager failed to teach.
- Thinking you're in each other's company all day so you already do one-on-ones; one-on-ones that are too short, prepared by the boss, or with the personal topics left to the last few minutes.
- The senior person speaks first and those who know the subject stay quiet; or peers probe the room and speak only once a consensus has formed.
- Taking someone's personal ability for their familiarity with a new task: the practitioner moved a star salesperson into running a factory, his performance dropped markedly, and soon after he was back at his old level.

## Two schools of thought

**1. Pick a few priorities and spot-check, versus see every side first**

**One school:** out of everything that looks important, take on just one or two (another source calls it finding the key problem); supervise by sampling and questions, not by digging into everything.

**The other school:** before acting, learn the specifics of each side. Hear one side, or glance from a distance and then act, and you will most likely cause a mess.

How to choose: the practitioner's own compromise is to dig into details at irregular intervals but with a budget on how often. (Judgment) Sample when the matter is reversible and narrow; look at everything first for big, irreversible decisions that touch several parties; and when time is short, "one more side" can postpone action forever, so set a line for how much you'll look at before you move.

**2. Decide promptly versus don't let the decision be born premature**

One side says delaying a decision is often the same as making a wrong one; the other says deciding before discussion is enough wastes the discussion. The practitioner's reconciliation: once the discussion has gathered enough relevant information, push toward consensus, and decide even if there's none. (Judgment) Judge "enough" by the deadline plus "have the people who should be asked all been asked?"

**3. A senior person in the room versus everyone echoing the moment the senior person speaks**

The two don't actually collide: the senior person's value lies in steering and giving confidence, not in speaking first. Joining those two statements into one is our own synthesis, not something in the source (judgment).

**4. Eliminating errors versus managing errors**

Another source, about software, argues that a complex system can't be tested exhaustively and that zero defects is a cost choice that trades execution efficiency for production efficiency; the job shifts from eliminating errors to managing them. Carrying that over to team decisions is an inference (judgment): don't expect to remove every error by stretching the discussion out forever. But managing errors is not tolerating them — you need a way to correct and weed out, and for irreversible decisions the extra time is worth it.

## When this doesn't work

- You are still a main hands-on producer (you write the code or close the big customers yourself): this arithmetic only applies fully once the team can carry the load (inference).
- The whole team is under six people: the headcount point works more as a reminder of how much time to set aside per person than as a staffing target (inference).
- You don't yet share an information base and a way of working: building that matters more than rushing to delegate; with a novice, teach first, then let go — delegate-plus-spot-check suits people who already have some familiarity.
- A founder is often both the person who knows most and the one who makes the final call, so the three steps shrink to two: let dissent really surface, and don't blur the decision once made (inference). The practitioner also admits the sequence is hardest for middle managers: it's hard to speak freely, and harder still to support a decision you disagree with.
- When every participant is a battle-hardened veteran, their confidence dissolves the go-in-circles problem.
- A one-on-one assumes the other person will tell you the truth; how to make that safe under a power gap, the sources don't say.

## Examples

- In a manager-training course at a large company, a group of managers of equal rank sat round a table to solve a problem while the slightly more senior chair was deliberately sent out. For nearly fifteen minutes nobody found the core of the problem, and nobody noticed they were going in circles; the chair came back, listened for a while, slapped the table to pull the talk back on topic, and it was settled quickly. This was a single classroom exercise with no control group.
- The same company had to pick a site for a plant expansion, and managers of equal rank on two lines argued opposite sides. The practitioner broke the deadlock with the six questions: two options, decide within a month, deciders being the heads of the two lines and their bosses, himself as the one to make the call, and the chairman to be informed afterwards. The people below studied it and chose the site near the old plant, and the practitioner approved after asking about sources and reasoning. This is the only instance, one where he was the decider and it went smoothly, with no example of the process going wrong.

## How solid is the evidence

- Almost all of it is one practitioner's long experience in one large, professional-heavy company with clear layers, plus a handful of examples; no samples, no controls.
- Every number (six to eight reports, the eight-person ceiling, a quarter, eighty per cent, at least an hour) is a rule of thumb; the cost-per-hour figure is from another era, so what transfers is the method. The "multiplier" has no measurement method and works better as a way of ranking than as a formula.
- The core evidence for "add a steering figure to a group of peers" is a single exercise of about fifteen minutes; "nine in ten bosses rate themselves too high" comes from an informal survey whose sample size is not given.
- The three-level style model rests on a behavioural-science summary, rotation observations and analogies, and the levels can only be read from the description; "process matters more than speed" is a claim, not a measured result.
- The "see every side" school argues entirely from quotations and one fictional episode — the weakest grade of evidence; it is credible because it matches common sense. "There must be one key problem" is an assertion that leaps from a few examples.

## One-page checklist

- [ ] I listed this week's activities and marked the one or two with the highest multiplier and the negative ones to stop
- [ ] Each direct report has a one-on-one; they write the agenda; commitments are written down afterwards
- [ ] For what I've handed off: scope is clear, and I've decided when, what and how often I'll check
- [ ] Before a decision meeting: I can state the decision I need, I've done the cost sum, there are no more than eight people, the agenda is out, and minutes will go out fast
- [ ] Before deciding I went through the six questions, especially "who can veto?"
- [ ] In discussion, people dared to disagree; the decision is clear; those who disagree have committed to carry it out
- [ ] For each person I chose instructions, communication, or only confirming direction, based on how well they know this task
- [ ] I asked my people how well they think I communicate and delegate
