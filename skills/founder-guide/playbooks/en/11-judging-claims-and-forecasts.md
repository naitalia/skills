# Judging Claims, Numbers and Forecasts

Many of the problems with numbers in a document aren't arithmetic. The source isn't explained, the comparison was picked to flatter, or the conclusion says one step more than the evidence does; and when forecasting, it is easy to be carried off by a story that merely sounds right. This card lines the questions up in one chain: who is saying it, what kind of evidence it is, whether the numbers were selected, what else could explain it, and how to make and judge a forecast. It gives you a degree of suspicion, not a veto, and certainly not a reason to throw out statistics wholesale.

## Quick take

- **Key questions**: What kind of evidence is this: intuition, a personal experience, a typical case, a testimonial, an expert's opinion, or statistics? What else could explain the same result, and what would have happened without this approach? If it's a forecast: what kind of question is it, how far out, and is the answer a number or just "likely"?
- **Preferred plays**: (1) Name the type of evidence: all of it gets called evidence, but strength varies enormously, mostly by type; for an expert, ask how much training and experience they have on this specific topic and whether they have first-hand access; for praise, ask about the people we never heard from and whether the endorser has a stake; intuition can't be inspected by anyone else, and confidence is not evidence; (2) Check the numbers: ask who the population is, whether the people who didn't answer are the same as everyone else, whether "average" means the mean or the median, what the base of a percentage is and what the raw counts are, and whether the vertical axis starts at zero; if you can't write down how a key figure was obtained, don't decide on it yet (judgment); (3) Inspect any forecast: ask what kind of question it is, whether the answer is a numerical probability or just "likely", how the forecaster revises on new evidence, and whether, after being wrong, he dissects what he did wrong or makes excuses; for extrapolations, ask whether "all else stays equal" and "the trend continues" hold over the forecast period and how much the result moves if you start elsewhere.
- **Common traps**: Assuming that coming from a professional body means it wasn't processed (before a number reaches you it may have been inflated, simplified or cherry-picked by sales staff, PR people, reporters or copywriters); using these questions only against views you disagree with, or finding one flaw and throwing the whole thing out, when the better move is to ask for sturdier evidence; diving into details and building a story that hangs together, then possibly getting anchored by a number that means little.
- **Evidence**: The statistics questions come from one proponent's long experience and individual cases, with no experimental or comparison data; most of the forecasting material is single observations and analogy, and the examples are high-scoring forecasters picked out to tell a story.
- **Not in this card**: A full method for sizing a market (how top-down and bottom-up estimates check each other); how to establish causation (how to run a comparison, how to design an A/B test or an early experiment).

## Ask these first

1. What am I being asked to believe? The more contested the claim, the harder the evidence has to be.
2. What does the claimant gain if I accept this? Does he know it first-hand, or is he relaying it?
3. What kind of evidence is this: intuition, a personal experience, a typical case, a testimonial, an expert's opinion, or statistics?
4. Do the numbers fit common sense and what I can see around me?
5. What else could explain the same result? What would have happened without this approach?
6. If it's a forecast: what kind of question is it, how far out, and is the answer a number or just "likely"?

If you only have time for two or three, the sources don't rank them; whether the evidence is hard enough and whether there's another explanation are, comparatively, the ones that most often settle it (judgment).

## Plays

### 1. Check where it came from: who says it, and how do they know?

The most dangerous bias is often the kind the claimant doesn't know he has, so asking only whether he's lying isn't enough.
1. Write the conclusion and the reasons in one sentence each and circle the vague key words. For each one, find two readings that both make sense, plug them in, and see whether the reasons still hold up the conclusion.
2. Write down what the claimant gets out of it: a researcher who needs to prove his theory, a press that wants a good story, people whose jobs are on the line, an adviser who profits from the action he recommends. If every discrepancy leans in his favour, be suspicious; though whether it is incompetence or intent, the source says, is hard to tell.
3. Data coming from an institution doesn't mean the conclusion came from that institution. When experts cite experts, reports cite reports, or sources go unnamed, trace back to the earliest claim and judge how reliable that one is.

### 2. Name the type of evidence: experts, testimonials and intuition each get their own questions

All of it gets called evidence, but strength varies enormously, mostly by type; once you know the type you know what to ask.
1. Expert opinion: how much training and experience does he have on this specific topic? Does he have first-hand access? Do experts with different positions and interests agree? Does his reputation come from a long record of reliable claims?
2. Praise and endorsements: what about the people we never heard from? Does the endorser have a stake in what he recommends? By what standard does he call it "good"? Do I believe it because of the evidence, or because he seems sincere?
3. Intuition: nobody else can inspect it, and when two intuitions clash there is no way to referee. Ask whether anything else stands behind it; whether this field has stable regularities; how many times he has practised this kind of judgment and how quickly he learns whether he was right. Confidence is not evidence, and meeting both conditions is necessary but not sufficient. The sources name long-run stock prices and political forecasting as environments without stable regularities; new-venture success or failure falls in the same class, so a founder's gut feeling that "this market will work" mostly shouldn't count as evidence (judgment, an inference stitched together from several sources).
4. Titles, degrees, CVs: being smart and well-informed seems to help, but beyond a certain range it adds little to forecasting accuracy. Turning that into questions for others is an inference; ask how he breaks the question down, what proportion among similar cases he starts from, what evidence would make him change his mind, and whether his forecasts have ever been seriously checked afterwards.

### 3. Check the numbers: how they were obtained, how they're stated, how they're drawn

The same data can be told in many legitimate ways, and the person telling it usually picks whichever suits him best.

How they were obtained (surveys, polls, pilots):
1. Who is the population? Did everyone have the same chance of being picked? However large the sample, a biased draw only gives you a more precise wrong answer.
2. How many were sent out and how many came back? Are the people who didn't answer, couldn't be reached, or volunteered the same as everyone else on the thing being measured?
3. Are people willing and able to tell the truth about this? Is there real purchase or usage data to check against? Who asked, and how?
4. How many people in total, and how many are left in each sub-group? How many times would the event you're watching be expected to show up? When that's tiny, even a "large" sample says nothing. How many times was it run, and were the unflattering runs put away? Is the difference bigger than sampling error, and does the report give an error margin?

How they're stated:
5. Is "average" the mean, the median or the mode? If it doesn't say, assume the mean, which in skewed data a few huge values drag upward. Average of whom? Only an average, with no range?
6. What is the base of the percentage, and what are the raw counts — could it be just two or three people? Is it percentage points or percent?
7. Which denominator does the ratio use (sales or investment, annual or half-year rate)? From what year does the growth start? Change the denominator, the base period or the metric: does the conclusion flip? Which comparable figure is missing?

How they're drawn:
8. Does the vertical axis start at zero? If it's cut, is that marked? Does the curve carry a scale and units? A curve with no numbers could be a doubling or a trivial rise. For icons and bubbles, is it height that scales, or area and volume? A 2-to-1 number can look like 4-to-1, even 8-to-1.

Finally:
9. Is it suspiciously precise? Does it fit common sense and what you see around you? Is it compatible with deeper regularities (the foundational disciplines such as economics and psychology)?
- Done when: for every figure that matters you can write down how it was obtained, who is being averaged, and what the base and base period are; if you can't, don't decide on it yet (judgment).

### 4. Look for other explanations: is the evidence measuring what the conclusion talks about?

The data can be true and the sample large, and the conclusion can still be unproven.
1. Is what was measured the same thing the conclusion talks about? What does the key word actually mean ("coverage", "improvement", "number one" — by which yardstick)? Are the two sides compared really comparable, absolute counts or rates?
2. Could the rise or fall come only from a change in definition, scope or the way things are recorded, or from some other factor that also changed between then and now? When the data comes from what people say, do they do what they say?
3. What would have happened without this approach? Is there a comparison group that didn't use it?
4. Two things move together: is it coincidence (a small sample, one favourable pair picked out), is the causation reversed or running both ways, is a third factor pushing both, or has the relationship simply never been observed in that range?
5. What other conclusions could reasonably follow from the same reasons? Write at least one more, and don't just pick the one you like. Once new evidence arrives, does the conclusion still hold?

### 5. Estimating a number or a probability yourself: break it down, start from the base rate, and only then talk about what's special about us

A number blurted out from the gut has no visible origin, and nobody can point to where it's wrong; write the guesses out in the open so they can be tested.

Estimating a quantity (market size, reachable customers, headcount needed):
1. First confirm you're answering the original question and not an easier stand-in.
2. Ask: which quantities would I need to know to get the answer? Break it into pieces that multiply or add up to the result, and mark each as known or unknown. For each unknown, where needed, first give a range you're about 90% sure contains the truth, take the middle, and write down the assumption behind it so others can point at the one that doesn't hold.
3. Compute the result, find a rough outside number to check it against, and see how far off you are and which piece looks shakiest.

Estimating a probability (success or failure, whether a round closes, whether you ship on time, whether a competitor enters):
4. Start from the base rate: how often does this kind of event happen in similar situations? Where several classes are available, pick the one more closely tied to your case. With no ready statistics, reason out a range from common sense, give one reason for each end, and take the midpoint.
5. Then look at this particular case: split "it happens" into a few hypotheses, list the conditions each needs, and look for evidence on both sides in a targeted way. Use what you find to move the starting point up or down, with a stated reason for every adjustment.
6. Seek other views: write your analysis out and ask someone to pick it apart; assume you are wrong, ask why, estimate again and merge the two; flip the question and look for arguments in the opposite direction.
7. Don't jump to "we're special": the source says nothing is 100% unique, uniqueness is a matter of degree, and even seemingly unique events can be given a class of reference cases (treating it as an alarm is a judgment).
- Done when: every unknown has a number and a range, and the final figure can be walked back step by step to its starting point.

### 6. Inspect any forecast before you accept it — someone else's, and your own

Someone else's forecast, your own forecast, and a number projected forward from the last few years all go through the same list. A title alone doesn't show a forecast is reliable; process and track record deserve more weight (judgment).
1. What kind of question is it? Where it runs like clockwork, a simple rule of thumb gets you close; where it's cloudlike and unpredictable, elaborate models may do no better than guessing; for something too far out (the result of an election more than a decade away), don't even try.
2. Is the answer a numerical probability or just "likely"? How many gradations are there, or is everything "fifty-fifty"? Leaving no room at all amounts to being willing to stake everything.
3. How does the forecaster revise when new evidence comes in? Good updaters mostly move in small steps but will take a big one when the signal is real. Does he acknowledge the counter-argument? Did he write down in advance what signals would turn him around?
4. After being wrong, does he dissect what he did wrong, or make excuses? After being right, did he check that the reasoning was actually sound, or just assume that being right meant the reasoning was? Is there a record of probabilities written down beforehand and scored against outcomes?
5. For extrapolations: do "all else stays equal" and "the trend continues" both hold over the forecast period? Which stretch of history gave the growth rate, and how much does the result move if you start elsewhere? Push the same line further out: does it become absurd?

## Common traps

- Assuming that coming from a professional body means it wasn't processed: before a number reaches you it may have been inflated, simplified or cherry-picked by sales staff, PR people, reporters or copywriters.
- Using these questions only against views you disagree with and never on your own or the ones you like; or finding one flaw and throwing the whole thing out, when the better move is to ask for sturdier evidence. These questions decide how far to trust, not who is guilty; distortion often comes from ignorance.
- Diving into details and building a story that hangs together, then possibly getting anchored by a number that means little; the base rate is abstract and dull, and even clever people tend to skip it.
- Using one framework for years and, when something doesn't fit, tending to assume there must be a good explanation you just haven't seen. The proponent says he has this blind spot too; the sources offer no repeatable way to spot it from inside.

## Two schools of thought

**Extrapolate, or use the base rate.** One school: the trend so far is fact, but the future trend is only an informed guess; extrapolation assumes conditions stay put and the trend carries on, and change the starting point and the result can move by orders of magnitude.
The other: start from how often similar events happened in the past, then adjust for the specifics.
How to choose (judgment): the dividing line is whether conditions have changed. In the second school's own example, the forecaster threw out older data precisely because the situation had changed. So you can start from past frequency, but first ask whether conditions have changed; if they have, drop the old data or adjust, and if you can't tell, don't treat the extrapolated number as a conclusion. For unprecedented events historical rates are even harder to use; the source advises leaning less on historical data, at the price of a greater risk of over-calling extreme outcomes.

**Look at expertise, or look at process.** One school: when judging an expert, first ask whether he has expertise on this topic.
The other: intelligence and knowledge seem to help, but beyond a certain range they add little to forecasting accuracy.
How to choose (judgment): expertise is the ticket to the room, not a pass to be believed; once in, look at how the person breaks the problem down and at his track record of being checked. The second school measured general intelligence and current-affairs knowledge, not industry expertise, so don't stretch it into "people who know the industry are no use".

**Distrust expert intuition, or train a method.** One school: environments such as long-run stock prices and political forecasting have no stable regularities, so experts are just as confident and just as wrong, and their intuitions can't be trusted (that new-venture success falls in this class is an inference).
The other: for questions of moderate difficulty, practising with a method and with feedback can do better.
How to choose (judgment): distrust intuition, trust method plus feedback; and on cloudlike questions, don't place bets.

**Compatibility with basic regularities, or agreement with consensus.** One school: ask whether a claim fits with surrounding and deeper regularities, for example a business model must be consistent with foundational disciplines such as economics and psychology. The other: agreeing with the group's beliefs is testing consensus against consensus, which is circular. (Judgment) The two schools come from the same proponent in two successive passages, and he never reconciles them; agreement with a large body of verified knowledge remains a weak indicator, though he treats that route as a pure flaw. How to choose (judgment): what you compare against must be a regularity that can be verified independently, not what peers say.

## When this doesn't work

- When you're handed digested numbers, these questions tell you where to dig; they can't replace recomputing from the raw data once you have it (judgment). The sources' examples are also almost all newspapers, polls and ads from the middle of the last century, so online surveys, self-selected samples and A/B tests need translating by you.
- If interviews are meant to find problems rather than estimate proportions, a dozen people is still worth having, and the sampling checklist shouldn't be used to dismiss it; likewise praise can show why someone likes a product but can't prove it works (both judgment).
- When data is roughly symmetric the different averages agree; a truncated axis that is clearly labelled isn't misleading; for charts that never start at zero anyway, such as financial prices, you must supply your own test. And even after you've pinned down the method, there may be no single correct number.
- Unprecedented events: base rates may not apply or be hard to choose. Whether a numerical probability for your own company's fate can be as reliable as one for a repeatable event, the source admits it cannot be tested.
- When the decision is small and reversible, going through the whole list isn't worth the cost (judgment).

## Examples

- For the same residents and the same income data, once to sell a house and once to oppose a tax rise, two "average incomes" were quoted that differed by more than fourfold, and both could be computed: one was the mean, the other the median. Lesson: an "average" that doesn't say which kind tells you almost nothing.
- Ownership of a new kind of household appliance rose by nearly ten thousand percent in five years; project the same rate for another five and every household would own dozens. Start from an earlier base year and you get a far smaller figure.
- A forecaster was asked whether a certain kind of event would happen in the coming two-plus months. Six cases in the past five years gave 1.2 a year; he dropped older data because the situation had changed and raised it to 1.5; then, because precautions had tightened, added a fifth for 1.8; multiplied by the window's share of a year to get 0.34, and reported 34%. The event did happen, and even its proponent concedes that pure intuition would have scored better. The 0.34 is really an expected count within the window; treating arrivals as random, the chance of at least one is roughly 29% (a conversion, not the source's claim). Lesson: the procedure doesn't guarantee a better score on any single question; its value is that every step can be challenged.

## How solid is the evidence

- The statistics questions come from one proponent's long experience and individual cases, with no experimental or comparison data; their value lies in the specificity of the checklist and counter-examples, not in proving any practice works. For the charts item, the original figures aren't visible and it rests on the proponent's verbal descriptions.
- Most of the forecasting material is single observations and analogy, and the examples are high-scoring forecasters picked out to tell a story. The anchoring finding and the "assume you're wrong and estimate again" finding each come from one controlled experiment, run by others and reported second-hand; the set of forecasting habits is a condensed guide whose evidence sits elsewhere. The break-it-down estimate has only one check that happened to land close, and errors in the pieces multiply (judgment).
- The data behind "smart and knowledgeable adds little" comes from people who volunteered for the proponent's own project and passed every test; he says they can't represent the population of any region, and it hasn't been independently replicated. "Specialist experts are only slightly better than avid readers" is a relayed impression, with no data.
- The "compatible with basic regularities" check: the proponent admits it takes professional training in logic to apply fluently; separately, it clashes with the circularity he criticised earlier, and he never reconciles the two (judgment).

## One-page checklist

- [ ] I can write down what they want me to believe, what they gain, and how they know
- [ ] For each key number: how it was obtained, who is averaged, the base and the base period
- [ ] For survey numbers I've checked response rate, sample size, expected event count and error margin; for charts, the axis start and the scale
- [ ] I've written at least one other explanation, and a comparison group that didn't use this approach
- [ ] My own estimate: broken into pieces, a range for each unknown, the base rate as the starting point, reasons and reversal signals written down
- [ ] For any extrapolated number: have conditions changed, and how much does it move with a different starting point?

## Not covered by this card

- A full method for sizing a market: how top-down and bottom-up estimates check each other, and what real data to use for the pieces
- How to choose the set of similar cases; what the success rate of comparable startups actually is (the sources give no figure and no way to choose the class)
- How to establish causation: how to run a comparison, how to design an A/B test or an early experiment
- How to ask the other side for raw data, and what to do if they refuse
- How the denominators and time windows of startup metrics (subscription revenue, renewals, retention, conversion) get chosen; the base period of growth rates in investor materials
- How to record probabilities, how to score them, how often to review; how to combine several people's forecasts
- There is no direct example for "how big will this industry be in three years" or "can we pull this off"; the sources' examples are mid-century media and current-affairs questions with deadlines
