# Positioning Against Stronger Competitors

When you collide with a big company, don't start by comparing resources. Ask what position each of you holds in the customer's mind, and where the other side can't move. When the category already exists, the way through is to find a position nobody holds in the customer's mind and then beat the incumbent inside a narrower group; when the market itself doesn't yet exist, it is to write your plan as a learning plan and leave yourself room for another try. These two approaches pull against each other, so step one is to judge which situation you are in.

## Quick take

- **Key questions**: Is the category already formed, with ready-made boxes in customers' minds, or is it a new market whose users don't yet know how to use the thing (judgment)? Is the position you want really empty in the customer's mind, and could they file you into an existing category in one sentence? Which assumptions in your plan do you actually know and which are guesses, and how many tries can your money pay for?
- **Preferred plays**:
  - **Size up the rival first: where it is strong, where it can't move**: look for the rival's most entrenched assumptions and interests (for example, its dealers and profit structure may make it hard to follow you into your spot; the sources give no test for when this reversal will really happen), estimate how long its pre-emption would take, and your window (judgment) is to get your position planted before that blocking takes effect.
  - **Take a position nobody holds in the customer's mind**: don't build a "better" copy; check whether the position is really empty in the customer's mind and whether they could file you under an existing category in a sentence, and be willing to give up some customers.
  - **When the market doesn't exist yet, write the plan as a learning plan**: list the critical assumptions and mark which are guesses, test the most critical ones before sinking expensive, irreversible money, and keep enough resources for a second and third attempt (the source doesn't say how much is enough).
- **Common traps**: Building a "better" copy while trying to please everyone; picking no single route and wanting to be cheap, special and broad all at once (you get squeezed from both ends); believing the first forecast for a new market and spending the budget in one go.
- **Evidence**: Mostly selected or after-the-fact-classified company cases plus structural reasoning, with no controlled data; "a market that doesn't exist can't be analysed" rests on one proponent line saying research has shown it (the figures are missing) plus three company cases, with survivorship bias.
- **Not in this card**: Once the big company is already in and cutting prices or copying you, what exactly a blocked challenger should do; how a B2B or software product finds its dimensions of "open position" and confirms in advance that the position is really open.

## Ask these first

1. Am I facing a category that is already formed, with ready-made boxes in customers' minds, or a new market whose users don't yet know how to use the thing? That decides which set of plays below applies (judgment).
2. Does the rival's strength come from scale and cost, or from the position it holds in customers' minds? Would its channel partners, profit structure and internal thinking let it follow me into the spot I'm taking?
3. Is the position I want really empty in the customer's mind? Could they file me into an existing category in one sentence?
4. Am I trying to be cheap, special and broad all at once? Am I willing to give up some customers?
5. Once the rival spots my concept, how fast can it cut prices or launch a separate brand to block me? Will anyone inside it drag their feet?
6. Which assumptions in my plan do I actually know, and which am I guessing? How many tries can my money pay for?

## Plays

### 1. Size up the rival first: where it is strong, where it can't move

A big company's strength is mainly the position its product holds in the customer's mind, not its size; entering late in a category someone else has already claimed usually fails. That "usually" comes from selected company stories, not a systematic sample.
1. Look for the rival's most entrenched assumptions and interests: its most solid ground is often its most fragile point. For example, if its dealers earn far more at the high end and feel small products harm the brand, it will struggle to follow you down into the low end where you are entering. The sources give no test for when this reversal will really happen.
2. Estimate its pre-emption: the sources' prescription for the big company is to block you before your concept has taken root in customers' minds, by cutting prices, launching a separate brand, or widening its name. Your window (judgment) is to get your position planted before that blocking takes effect. The proponent also observed that most leaders are slow, dismiss rivals' progress, and treat new businesses as threats to the old one; that is where you can bet, but it is a probability, not a guarantee. And every pre-emption example he gives is one where the leader won.
3. Don't treat the rival as fixed: where it has both the ability and the motive to adjust, your moves change its reactions, except for things that really are fixed, such as regulation or physical limits. And don't mistake relative standing for your absolute condition: beating a rival doesn't mean you're winning, since in a non-zero-sum game you can be stronger than the rival while both of you get worse off; but many situations in practice are close to zero-sum (fixed budgets, a single post), so first judge which kind you are in.
- Done when: you can write down how the rival would try to block you, how long it would take, who inside would drag their feet, and which of its own interests keep it from following you (judgment).

### 2. Don't build a "better" copy; take a position nobody holds in the customer's mind

What works for the leader doesn't necessarily work for the follower; a copycat product that emphasises being better over being fast misses its sales targets; copying sometimes works, but only when the leader hasn't managed to establish its position in time.
1. Run the five checks: is the position in the customer's mind or only on my production line; has someone else already built the perception; could they file me into an existing category in a sentence (the first clear whisky didn't count as first in customers' minds, since clear spirits such as gin and vodka already existed; it sold a third of forecast); does the position require them to change deep-rooted beliefs; am I trying to please everyone again.
2. Look for entry points along dimensions: size, price (high or low), gender, age, time of day, channel, heavy users. When everyone in the industry heads one way, the opportunity is often in the opposite direction. The examples are consumer brands; in B2B, swap in industry, company size and use case (inference).
3. A premium position must be backed by real differentiation and you should be the first to establish it; new products often suit a low-price position, while for old products, especially where customers are unhappy with repair service, a premium is often better.
4. Be willing to give up some customers: with so many competitors, trying to satisfy everyone simply can't be done.
5. Once you hold the position: timing beats a good idea (a new brand about twenty years late had to be abandoned); don't stretch the brand into other businesses; give up an established position and share collapses.
- Done when: you can say in one sentence "I'm the first X for Y", and a prospect hearing it won't file you under an existing category.

### 3. When resources can't cover the whole industry: pick one route and commit; if you're pinned down, change the battlefield

Overall cost leadership, differentiation, and focusing on one group need different resources, skills and organisation, so choose one and stick with it for the long run; do none of them properly and you get squeezed from both ends.
1. Elimination: cost leadership needs high share, heavy upfront investment and a willingness to absorb start-up losses, so for a startup with neither share nor capital it is basically closed, leaving differentiation and focus (judgment).
2. Choosing focus: pick a narrower buyer group, product category or region, possibly one where rivals are weakest and substitutes fewest, and rebuild every function around it. My reading is that this is operational detail, not a positioning slogan (judgment; for example, a specialist paint supplier gave painters a free coffee room and delivered even a single gallon quickly). The premise is that you really can serve this group better or more cheaply than a broad-line rival.
3. Accept the price: share is often capped, profit and volume must be traded off; the risks are a widening cost gap with broad-line players, the target group's needs converging with the whole market's, and rivals finding finer segments inside your group.
4. When a strong brand has you pinned at home, you can abandon that ground, put your last money into one point the big players haven't covered, and then use the win there to build momentum. What transfers is the structure, not the tactics of a period when the rules were in disarray.

### 4. Emerging industries: don't rush to wall off rivals; first judge whether going early pays

1. If the bottleneck lies in shared problems such as credibility, standards and buyer doubt, the players' fortunes are interdependent. A pioneer shouldn't pour too many resources into guarding share or reacting to rivals unlikely to become mainstays; put the effort into shaping the rules and pushing standards (weigh standardisation against keeping your product distinctive; it is not always right to push), and where useful invite particular rivals in through licensing and the like. An occasional fierce response is still warranted.
2. Early or wait: check the conditions. Favouring early entry: reputation matters to buyers; what you learn is hard to copy and won't be wiped out by successive technology changes; customers are loyal; locking up raw materials and channels early gives a cost edge. Favouring waiting: early competitive and segmentation patterns are quite unlike later ones; the cost of opening the market is high and the gains can't be yours alone; the small rivals you fight early are soon replaced by more fearsome later arrivals; technology change makes early investment obsolete. The conditions are listed without weights.
3. Early barriers mainly come from risk tolerance, innovation ability and judgment, with the size of resources secondary; as the industry matures the barriers fade quickly and large firms arrive with economies of scale.
4. Judge who will buy first (this only works when the buyer groups already exist; judgment): how pressing the performance advantage is for them; how big the cost advantage is; how costly a failure of the product would be (the higher, the more hesitant); how costly switching is; whether they see technology change as an opportunity or a threat; and how much personal risk the decision-maker bears if they choose wrong. Don't look at performance advantage alone.
5. Where the market already exists, replace a single-point forecast with several self-consistent scenarios; if you have ample resources, work to bring about the most favourable one, and if resources are limited or uncertainty is obvious, stay flexible.

### 5. When the market doesn't exist yet: write the plan as a learning plan

A market that doesn't exist can't be analysed, its uses are unknowable as it forms, and in such markets the first strategy is generally wrong.
1. List every assumption the plan depends on (who the users are, what they use it for, how much demand), and mark which are guesses.
2. Before sinking expensive, irreversible money (a dedicated production line, special design features), test the most critical assumptions; design with modules that are easy to reconfigure.
3. Watch how users actually use it rather than listening to what they say; keep an eye out for successes outside the plan, because new markets often grow from there.
4. Keep enough resources for a second and third attempt, or a relationship with backers and investors who trust you: one idea failing shouldn't mean the company fails. The source doesn't say how much is enough.
- Done when: every critical assumption has a way of being tested, and the budget has not been spent in one go.

## Two schools of thought

**One school: pick a position first, then commit.** The positioning school wants you to take a position in the customer's mind, giving up some customers and accepting losses to do it; the choose-one-route school wants you to pick a single approach and hold it for the long run, and to do none is to be stuck in the middle.
**The other school: a new market can't be analysed in advance; you can only learn as you go.** If the market doesn't exist, there is nothing to analyse first; the opening strategy is generally wrong, and success depends on who survives to a second and third attempt: the proponent says a company that uses up its resources or credit before it finds a viable strategy is a failing one.
Scenario analysis sits in between (judgment): it swaps a single forecast for several self-consistent scenarios, but it too leaves itself an out: when resources are limited or uncertainty is obvious, stay flexible.

The disagreement falls in three places: whether a position is claimed first or found by trying; whether the approach is chosen and held, or the first choice is probably wrong; whether to analyse scenarios beforehand or whether there is nothing to analyse.

How to choose (judgment): look at whether the category already exists in customers' minds. If it is formed, with known users and comparable rivals, choose a position and a single route and commit, without backing several at once. If it doesn't exist and users don't know how to use the product, treat the position as a hypothesis to be tested, and keep resources for a second try. Real products often fall between the two, and you have to judge how to classify yours. Staking your last money on one point conflicts with keeping resources in reserve, and the dividing line is probably again whether you are still searching for the position.

Three smaller disagreements turn on different conditions (judgment): whether scale counts as strength (the positioning school says position does; the choose-one-route school counts economies of scale as one source of cost advantage); being first versus going early only on conditions (see Play 4); and pre-empting versus not walling off rivals (the former is a leader's quick reply when a new concept appears in a category; the latter is resource allocation when an emerging industry's growth is held back by shared bottlenecks).

## When this doesn't work

- If the rival's pre-emption lands before you have taken root, a big company entering late can still win; change can also cost a leader its position.
- The examples of finding an open position are almost all American consumer brands from the 1970s to the 1990s, and the open spaces were identified in hindsight; whether it holds in software or platform markets you have to verify yourself.
- The choose-one-route scheme doesn't fit every industry: in commodities everything is a cost contest, and low cost doesn't necessarily conflict with differentiation. In the source's examples, being stuck in the middle is a diagnosis after the fact, not a test you can apply beforehand (judgment).
- Positions aren't permanent: time erodes established premium positions.
- A learning plan holds only where the market truly doesn't exist; in known markets the source itself says planning and research are what count.
- An early-stage system with no entrenched assumptions has no weak point to hit (judgment).

## Examples

- A drinks company far larger than the incumbent launched a similar drink in a category someone else had already claimed, and its sales stayed below about a sixth of the incumbent's. The evidence is a few selected stories.
- A foreign maker of small motorcycles, following its research, designed a fast, powerful bike for an unfamiliar large market and was nearly sunk by warranty costs. Its three staff on the ground rode small-engine bikes themselves, and neighbours asking where to buy one became the start of the turn; they argued with head office many times before it agreed, and only afterwards did engineering and manufacturing drive prices steadily down. A long-established premium local brand tried to follow with imported small-engine models and was blocked by its own dealers: the high end paid far more, and they felt small bikes hurt the brand's image. The account is relayed from people involved, the figures are missing, and it is a company that succeeded in the end.
- A big company's miniature storage-drive project set a very high three-year revenue target and, following forecasts, bet on production capacity and shock-resistant design; in its first two years it sold only a small fraction of forecast, the biggest applications were all outside the plan, and when the real demand came there were no resources left. The project manager later said his worst mistake was always believing his own forecast.

## How solid is the evidence

- The positioning school's case is selected company stories and one chart that gives only the result, about consumer goods and office equipment; how to spot open positions in advance is barely addressed; the list of entry points is grouped from cases, with no ranking and no conditions of use.
- The choose-one-route cases are industrial companies from around the 1970s, classified after the fact; even the proponent admits the relationship doesn't hold in every industry, and gives counterexamples. The focus examples are also after-the-fact descriptions of companies that succeeded; how to choose the group and confirm you serve it better, the source doesn't say.
- On emerging industries, the advice about walling off rivals and about timing rests on structural reasoning plus a few industry examples, with no data comparing walling off to opening up; the timing conditions carry no weights and don't consider whether a startup's cash can last until the market grows.
- "A market that doesn't exist can't be analysed" rests on one line saying research has shown it (the figures are missing) plus three company cases, with survivorship bias; the figures in the motorcycle case are missing too.
- "The firmest ground is the most fragile" is a powerful line, but the source gives no test for when it flips. Another claim, that one should think things through in abstract logic before acting, rests mainly on an example with historical errors; it is set aside and not used here to support the analyse-first school.
- Staking the last money on one city: the narrator attributes part of the success to the disorderly rules of that period, and companies that gambled the same way and failed are not in the account.

## One-page checklist

- [ ] I've judged whether the category is already formed in customers' minds or the market doesn't exist yet
- [ ] I can write "I'm the first X for Y" and have asked whether customers would file me under the old category
- [ ] I bet on only one of low price, distinctiveness or breadth, or have chosen a narrow group, and I know who I'm giving up
- [ ] I've estimated how fast the rival can block me and how much internal resistance it faces
- [ ] Emerging industry: I've checked both the go-early and the wait conditions, and thought about who buys first
- [ ] New market: critical assumptions are listed with ways to test them, and the budget isn't spent in one go
