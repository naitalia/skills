# Direction and Demand Validation

The most common mistake in validating demand is treating kind words as evidence: your own feeling, friends cheering you on, an interviewee saying "I want that" — none of it counts. This card rests on two judgments. First, watch what people actually do (how they cope today, whether they will pay or put a commitment in writing) rather than listen to what they say they want. Second, pin the direction to a specific person, a specific moment and a specific alternative they use now, instead of stopping at a broad need. For how to tell whether a launched product has found its market, see "MVP and Product Trade-offs".

## Quick take

- **Key questions**: Can you say who it's for, at what moment, and what they're trying to get done? How do they cope today, and what would they have to give up before switching to yours? Is your "evidence" your own feeling and friends cheering you on, or what people you don't know actually do?
- **Preferred plays**:
  - **Write the direction as a scene with context**: describe one specific person at one specific moment and what they're trying to get done, before you size the market.
  - **Check what they do today**: test what they say against what they've actually done; if they say they'd pay, ask for a small deposit.
  - **Problem interviews**: use the same outline every session, and first write what you want to test as hypotheses that can be proven wrong.
- **Common traps**: Turning the interview into a pitch (customers are likely to pretend to agree); treating "I'm my own customer" or "my friends like it" as validation; stopping price validation at a verbal yes.
- **Evidence**: Mostly the proponents' long experience plus single cases, with no controls and no independent replication; interview counts and pacing are personal experience, not derived thresholds.
- **Not in this card**: When to abandon the direction (the sources give only conditions for "enough to move on"); how to find and schedule the first interviewees when you have no network.

## Ask these first

1. Who is this for, at what moment, and what are they trying to get done? If you can't say, is all you have a broad phrase like "more efficient"?
2. How do they cope today: a poor product, several things patched together, or nothing at all? Is the problem painful enough to make them act? What would they have to give up before switching to mine?
3. Whose voice is my "evidence": my own feeling, friends cheering me on, one vivid story, or what people I don't know actually do?
4. For the field or user group I'm judging, have I dealt with it first-hand, or have I only read material someone else compiled?

## Plays

### 1. Write the direction as a scene with context, before you size the market

A broad need fits almost everyone and isn't enough to make anyone pick you over someone else; in this approach's view, what does the work is what one person is trying to get done in a concrete situation, and demographic profiles (age, income, region) can't predict whether someone will buy (this is the proponent's long experience, not a controlled finding).
1. Write down the progress they want (functional, social and emotional), and the context where the difficulty arises: who, when, where, doing what, and what stage of life they're in.
2. Write down the obstacles they hit along the way, what they patch together now, how they define "better", and what they'd give up to get it.
3. List everything else competing for the same job, including things outside your own industry.
- Done when: you can tell a short story about one specific person at one specific moment, not a demographic label (judgment).

### 2. Check what they do today, not just what they say they want

Customers saying untrue things in interviews is common — sometimes out of politeness to you, sometimes because they don't understand or don't care about the topic. Your job isn't to catch them lying; it's to check their words against what they've actually done.
1. When they say a problem must be solved, ask how they solve it now and have them walk you through the steps.
2. If they've done nothing and life goes on fine, the problem may be less serious than they say. But that is a clue, not a verdict — ask whether they never thought of it or tried and gave up.
3. If they're using a homemade fix or a competitor and are unhappy with it, the problem may be worth solving. If they call the problem important but have never looked for a solution, that's a contradiction — don't let it slide.
4. If they say they'd pay, ask for a small deposit and promise a full refund if they're not satisfied.
5. If you have purchase data, also check whether people kept using the product after buying: seeing only the moment of purchase hides whether the product actually got the job done.

### 3. See what they must give up first: push, pull, habit, anxiety

For something new to be adopted, the user must first give up an existing way of coping — even if that way is doing nothing. The pull of the new option has to outweigh old habit plus anxiety; and if the problem is merely annoying rather than painful enough to push them, even a strong pull may not get you considered.
1. Write down the existing practice they'd have to drop, then ask about each force: push (is the pain bad enough to act?), pull (does the new option make life clearly better?), habit (what lets them keep putting up with it?), anxiety (what do they fear — cost, learning something new, the unknown, "what if it's worse than the old way"?). When resistance is strong, find ways to weaken it and keep the cost of switching as low as you can.
- An old way that only served a functional purpose is not hard to give up, so resistance is probably lighter (judgment); things tied up with emotion and social standing are much harder to drop. The four forces are described only qualitatively in the source, so you have to judge their strength yourself.

### 4. Problem interviews: one outline, falsifiable hypotheses first

The aim is to find out whether problem and customer group fit. Using the same outline each time, with results you can repeat, is what makes it a systematic method.
1. Turn each thing you want to test into a sentence that can be proven wrong: a repeatable behavior will lead to an expected result. And decide in advance what interview outcome would disprove it (judgment).
2. Start recruiting from people you know who match the target profile and ask them for introductions. Use acquaintances only to practice the outline, then add people who don't know you.
3. Keep each session to twenty or thirty minutes. Pay nothing and offer no perks, because the people you want are ones who would pay you. Bring a companion to catch what you miss and to keep your own optimism in check.
4. Open by saying there's no product yet and you're not selling. Ask a few background questions to segment people. In the core of the session, go through the problems one by one, asking how they handle each today, have them go into detail, then stop talking and listen — no leading, no telling them how serious the problem is.
5. Right after each session, fill in notes against the hypotheses. Don't change the outline during the week; revise it after reviewing at week's end: keep the groups that resonate, drop those that don't, delete problems everyone calls unimportant, and narrow down to one problem that matters a lot.
- Done when: at least 10 interviews, and all three of these hold — you can describe the kind of person who would use it first, you've found one problem that matters a lot, and you can state clearly how customers solve it today. The 10, and 30–60 for the whole phase, are the proponent's personal experience, not derived thresholds.

### 5. A different way to ask: rebuild the timeline of a purchase

This suits purchases that look impulsive, or cases where you don't yet know what the problem looks like (the latter is judgment): skip product details and ask the person to walk you through everything from the first thought to buying and using.
1. Find someone who has just made this purchase or change. Start from the day they bought, then work backwards: when the thought first appeared, what was wrong with the old way, what they tried, why they put it off. Watch for painful moments, awkward trade-offs, workarounds and obstacles in the purchase. Lay several timelines side by side to find patterns rather than breaking each interview into categories.
- The proponent says the demonstration interview was deliberately slow and is not a universal template; in it, one question had the interviewer talk through the sequence for the interviewee first — don't copy that (judgment). And treat your gut feeling that you've "got it" as a hypothesis to test (judgment).

### 6. Take a demo out to test the solution, the price and commitment

Do this round only after problem interviews have turned up one important problem and a pool of candidate early users.
1. Build a demo that represents the real product. Walk through it problem by problem, pausing after each, and ask which features impressed them, which are optional, and what's missing. If the reaction is lukewarm, go back to the problem-interview outline.
2. Test price: don't ask customers to name one — state the price yourself and watch the reaction at once, noting whether they accept after hesitating or immediately. The best price is often one the customer resists slightly but can accept; if you meet no resistance, try nudging it up; if they use a free alternative now, ask whether your value is enough to make them give up free.
3. Get commitment in writing or as a small prepayment where you can; don't stop at a spoken "sure".
- Done when: you've pinned down the profile of the earliest users, found a problem that hurts a lot, are clear what the first version must solve, have settled the price customers will pay, and have roughly checked from current data that the company can survive. Move on only when all five are done.

## Common traps

- Turning the interview into a pitch: when you do most of the talking, customers are likely to pretend to agree, make things up, or hide what they really think.
- Treating "I'm my own customer" or "my friends like it" as validation: a founder can't be an objective customer, and whether friends skew rosy or gloomy depends on how they feel about startups.
- Stopping price validation at a verbal yes — or making the demo so polished that interviewees may be reacting to the craftsmanship rather than to their own problem (judgment; the proponent himself argues that a more realistic demo gives a more accurate test).
- Changing the whole team's direction because of one or two vivid in-depth interview stories (judgment: an easy mistake to make); a single experience or case shows only that something can happen, not that it is representative.

## Two schools of thought

**How to run the interviews.** One school: the same outline in every session, revised only weekly, so results are comparable. The other: dig deeply into a few people's whole story and lay the timelines side by side to see patterns; it holds that insight owes more to depth than to breadth, and says plainly that there is no universal template.
How to choose (judgment — neither source says this): if you don't yet know what the problem looks like, dig into timelines; if you already have hypotheses to test, use a fixed outline. Either way a deep interview yields only a hypothesis to check, and "depth over breadth" is no excuse to talk to just three or five people.

**One-on-one, or a room full of people.** One school: at the start avoid questionnaires and focus groups — the first assumes you already know what to ask, and in the second people soon fall in line with each other. The other: gather at least three people who know the situation well and have them debate one central question face to face, with the investigator present in person and taking their own notes.
How to choose (judgment): the difference may not be headcount but whether a facilitator puts every view on the table and then makes people confront each other. For the truth about one person's behavior, go one-on-one; to check a fact that people tell differently, use a facilitated discussion. If attendees hold back out of fear of those with power, the discussion fails, and the source doesn't deal with that.

**Hypothesis first, or go out first.** One school: investigating is itself how the problem gets solved — once the material is in, the answer surfaces. The other: write falsifiable hypotheses first, then test them. How to choose (judgment): they complement each other — hypotheses guard against seeing only what you hoped to see, going out guards against arriving with your conclusion ready; but when the difficulty is conflicting goals rather than unclear facts, no amount of material produces an answer.

**How to segment people.** One school: user characteristics don't predict behavior, and slicing experience into "men/women" or "big/small firms" destroys its meaning. The other: an investigation needs an analytical framework — the original looks at social classes and how they relate, and what carries over is mainly the point that you need a framework — or it becomes a pile of notes. How to choose (judgment): the second is about relationships between groups, not demographic tags, so part of the clash is only on the surface; demographic profiles are fine for finding who has the job, not for predicting demand.

## When this doesn't work

- Users have no real difficulty, or existing solutions are already good enough: the proponent says the framework is useless there; the same goes for decisions made almost entirely by trading-style math.
- Products that satisfy desire rather than relieve pain (games, short films, novels): no need to validate a problem as such; understand the audience first, then run quick small tests of the solution.
- Products used only once: the split between buying and repeated use does little, and you need another way to tell whether the job got done (judgment).
- Information you can't get, or no time to get it: you still have to decide on incomplete information, and the source gives no rule for that.
- The tricks of high prices, limited places and not making sign-up too easy rest on a single self-reported business-software conversation; whether they work for consumer products you'll have to test yourself.

## Examples

- A founder bought a mattress at a big-box store just before checkout, which looked like an impulse buy. The interviewer pulled the timeline backwards and found he'd been unhappy with the old mattress for about a year and put off buying because other things were consuming him; on the day, his spouse was there and the store took returns, which, in the proponent's reading, removed the last obstacle. Lesson: looking only at the moment of purchase can mistake a long build-up for impulse. But it was one interview, a rewritten transcript, with a few leading questions from the interviewer — an illustration only.
- A founder building a tool for parents to share children's photos and videos with relatives ran a dozen or so problem interviews and twenty or so solution interviews. Results: the real competitor was the free email parents already made do with; the bigger pain was video — parents weren't unwilling to share, they didn't know how and had tried and given up; the earliest users narrowed to first-time mothers of children under three; and price only reached a verbal "I'd give it a try". The numbers are his own count, and interviewees mostly came by introduction from people he knew. When he later pushed to a larger group, the target users were too busy to pay attention to the product — the same mothers had kept cancelling and rescheduling interviews, a warning nobody took seriously at the time.

## How solid is the evidence

- The claims in these sources are mostly proponents' long experience plus single cases, with no controls and no independent replication; interview counts and pacing are personal experience, and "focus groups quickly fall in line" is an assertion with no example.
- "What users do diverges from what they say" rests mainly on rhetorical examples and one single success story ("the focus group said no, it sold hugely"), which doesn't license "ignore users who say no". The half worth keeping is that purchase data alone misses use, though that too rests only on the proponent's reasoning.
- "It makes innovation predictable" rests on analogies and cases told with hindsight; in one corporate case one of the proponents had worked with that company, so discount it.
- The discussion-meeting method comes from experience of a rural survey many decades ago, with no controls; one or two vivid cases or experiences show something can happen, not that it is representative.

## One-page checklist

- [ ] I've described the job through one specific person at one specific moment, not a demographic label
- [ ] For each interviewee I recorded "what they do today", not "what they said they want"
- [ ] I've written down what they'd have to give up, plus their answers on push, pull, habit and anxiety
- [ ] Hypotheses are falsifiable, every session uses the same outline, and I change it only after a week
- [ ] I name the price myself and watch the reaction, and get commitment in writing or prepaid where possible

## Not covered by this card

- When to abandon the direction: the sources give conditions for "enough to move on" but nothing on how many noes, or what pattern, means stop
- Whether the direction is big enough, and how to size the market
- How to find and schedule the first interviewees when you have no network
- How to design the questionnaire that follows interviews, and how big the sample should be
- What to do when the buyer isn't the user, or several people decide (the source offers only one line: your customer's customer is the real customer)
- How to transcribe, sort and analyze many interviews into patterns
- These approaches took shape about a decade ago and have no examples for today's newer kinds of product
