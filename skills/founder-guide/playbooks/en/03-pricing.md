# Pricing

This card is about how buyers read a price. It does not tell you what number to put on a new product; the sources don't cover that. The core judgment: buyers carry no fixed price tag in their heads, and what they feel shifts with the reference point, the framing and the setting. Several of these effects have also come out much weaker when later researchers re-ran them, so treat every move below as a hypothesis to test on your own pricing page, not a lever that is guaranteed to work.

## Quick take

- **Key questions**: Does the buyer have a reference price of their own for this kind of thing (for someone who buys it often or knows the field, your number can shift their range only a little)? Can the buyer walk away, go elsewhere or compare prices directly (most of the experimental subjects in the sources could not)? Is your "willingness to pay" a single number people said out loud, or money they actually paid?
- **Preferred plays**:
  - **Don't treat a stated willingness to pay as a fixed price tag**: a survey or interview number is only a reading that depends on how you asked; ask about the same offer again in different words, and if the reaction changes, part of what you measured was the wording, not the preference (judgment).
  - **Designing tiers**: a third option may shift share, but treat it only as a hypothesis, test it on your own pricing page, and compare real purchases, not choices on paper.
  - **Before raising or cutting a price, check the reference point**: write down the buyer's baseline and whether your move will read as a gain or a loss; the sources do not apply this framework to concrete pricing moves, so this step is inference.
- **Common traps**: Treating "show a high price and willingness to pay goes up" as proven; using multiples from experiments as pricing parameters; transplanting it unchanged to subscriptions, software or business customers.
- **Evidence**: One of the firmer parts is numerical anchoring (it clearly appeared in a later multi-lab replication, but it measures estimating a quantity, not a number on a pricing page); irrelevant numbers pulling willingness to pay came out weak when re-run, the third-option effect appears reliably only when attributes are shown as numbers, gains and losses relative to a reference point are still disputed for everyday price reactions, and the rest is mostly practitioners' accounts, thought experiments or small experiments.
- **Not in this card**: What number to put on a new product (cost-plus, value-based pricing and competitor benchmarking are not covered in the sources); how to carry out a price rise (how much, how to talk to existing customers).

## Ask these first

1. Does the buyer have a reference price of their own for this kind of thing? For someone who buys it often or knows the field, your number can shift their range only a little; for someone buying for the first time with no sense of the cost, the number you show is what defines "reasonable".
2. Will the buyer file your price move (raise, cut, withdraw an offer, add a freebie) as a gain or a loss? What is their baseline right now: the price they expected, one they've seen before, or what competitors charge?
3. At the moment they decide, is your number still in their head? Has it been set next to the thing they're being asked to value?
4. Can the buyer walk away, go to someone else, or compare prices directly? Most of the experimental subjects in the sources could not, and that decides how much of an effect survives (judgment).
5. Where did your "willingness to pay" figures come from: a single number people said out loud, or money they actually paid?

## Plays

### 1. Check whether the buyer has a reference price before choosing which number to show first

When buyers have a ready reference price, your number can shift them only within a narrow range; when they don't, the price itself may be the clue.
1. Judge how familiar the buyer is: a regular who knows the field, or someone buying something new, a one-time visitor, or a rarely bought item.
2. If they have no reference price, the first number you show, the top tier, the setting and the product spec may all be defining what counts as a fair price for them.
3. Check that the number survives until the decision: important decisions get reconsidered repeatedly, so the number has to be memorable enough to come back each time. Also check whether it has been compared with the thing being valued: when buyers estimate "what is this worth?", numbers pull them in, while comparing it with some other item can push them away. These conditions are stitched together from several researchers' claims and form a checklist, not a prediction formula; the point that important decisions get reconsidered cites a field study in one sentence, with no source given.
- Done when: you can say whether the buyer has a reference price; if not, what your first number is and whether they'll see it again before deciding.

### 2. Don't treat a stated willingness to pay as the buyer's fixed price tag

The same buyer's bid moves with the question's wording, the description and even irrelevant numbers (the last of these came out weak when re-run), and they often don't know it.
1. Treat a number from a survey or interview as a reading that depends on how you asked, not as a price tag (judgment).
2. Ask about the same offer again in different words. If the reaction changes, part of what you measured was the wording, not the preference (judgment).
3. In the experiments, the same people's relative valuations of different items were fairly stable while their absolute bids were easily dragged around (this half came out weaker when re-run). The first half was not directly challenged by later re-runs, but it has not been checked line by line either.
4. The first price you quote early users may become the baseline for their later comparisons; keep that in mind when you lay out prices for the whole line (judgment).
- Done when: for every willingness-to-pay figure you hold, you can say how it was obtained and whether you've tried rewording the question.

### 3. Designing tiers: a third option can shift share, but treat it as a hypothesis to test

In one beer-choice experiment, adding an option almost nobody chose pushed choices toward the tier the experimenters wanted to sell.
1. Decide which tier you want more buyers to pick. To push the cheaper one, put something cheaper and worse below it; to push the premium one, put something pricier and only slightly better above it.
2. The decoy should itself attract few buyers. It is more likely to work when buyers are trading price against quality and can't judge quality themselves; when they can try the product, it often fails.
3. Notice how buyers justify their choice: those who picked the middle tier said the cheapest might be bad and the dearest might be a rip-off, so the middle felt safe.
4. Test it on your own pricing page and measure real purchases, not choices on paper.
- Done when: you have a real-purchase comparison of "with this tier" versus "without it" (judgment).

### 4. Before raising or cutting a price, check the reference point

Whether a buyer counts something as a gain or a loss depends on their baseline, and the same person can flip from cautious to risk-seeking when the baseline changes (applying this to price moves is inference; see step 2).
1. Write down the buyer's baseline right now: the price they expected, one they've seen before, or competitors' prices.
2. Judge whether your move will be filed as a gain or a loss. The sources do not apply this framework to concrete pricing moves, so this step is inference, and the buyer's baseline is invisible; you have to ask or test.
3. Check whether the seller is locked onto an old peak: after a bubble burst, sellers anchored on peak prices, so selling at the going market price felt like a loss; few deals closed and adjusting may take years (the source is only a narrative with no figures).
4. Faced with a sure loss, people tend to gamble rather than accept a smaller certain loss; the same pull may make someone hold on to an old price rather than cut it (the second half is judgment).
- Done when: for every price move you've written down the buyer's baseline and whether it will read as a gain or a loss, and marked each as inference or as something you asked or tested.

### 5. Check the signals a price sends, and before raising prices see what buyers remember

1. When buyers are short of time and have nothing but price to go on, a tier that is too cheap may read as "something's wrong", while the top tier lifts perceived value and makes other prices look like a bargain. This is only practitioners' own account plus the commentator's interpretation, with no controlled comparison.
2. If you want to price above competitors, ask first what buyers use to judge "fair": the same price reads as a rip-off in a plain setting and as fair in one with extra amenities. The sources offer only a thought experiment and the commentator's observations; none of it was tested.
3. Before raising prices, split your price items into those bought constantly, whose prices buyers remember well, and those bought rarely, where buyers neither remember the last price nor know the cost; pick candidates from the second group first. A retail practitioner's account: watch the few hundred best sellers closely and find margin on the obscure items.
4. Buyers not noticing is not the same as buyers never finding out; the sense of being fleeced when they do may eat the extra profit (judgment). This is an offline-retail practice, and in subscriptions or software it is unclear which charges count as "rarely bought", so judge from your own data.
- Done when: candidate items are sorted into "remembered / not remembered", and you've decided which few to test in a small way first rather than raising everything at once.

## Common traps

- Treating "show a high price and willingness to pay goes up" as proven. What the sources call anchoring is really three kinds of evidence: compare-then-estimate a quantity (replicated most strongly), irrelevant numbers pulling willingness to pay (weaker when re-run), and price read as a quality cue (no controlled comparison).
- Using multiples from experiments as pricing parameters. Doubling the feeling took several times the price in ratings, and the shift in tier shares came from choices on paper; neither involved real purchases. The high-number group bid more than three times as much as the low-number group in an auction where winners really paid, but a later re-run found only very weak anchoring. The sources never turn any of this into tier spacing.
- Transplanting all this unchanged to subscriptions, software or business customers: the sources' examples are almost all retail, auctions, mock juries and beer.

## Two schools of thought

**Group one: when the price rises, does the feeling barely move, or is the price itself the quality signal?**

**One school: the feeling grows more slowly than the amount.** When students rated watches, doubling the appeal took a price several times higher, so, by this reading, adding cost in a straight line, or persuading with "pay double, get double the value", may not match how buyers feel (judgment: the source does not take this step).

**The other school: when buyers have nothing else to go on, price gets read as quality.** Perceived value rises with price, and the lowest tier looks suspect.

How to choose (judgment): the two are about different things, one the feel of an amount and the other inferring quality from price. When buyers have no other basis for judging (something new, a one-time visitor), use the second and check what your cheapest tier signals; when they know the category and can compare, use the first, don't expect a price rise to buy a proportional lift in feeling, and test tier by tier.

**Group two: is an aggressive opening price punished?**

**One school: no.** In mock-jury experiments, the claim went from about a hundred dollars up to a billion and awards kept rising, though by less and less at the top; no evidence of retaliatory lowballing was found.

**The other school: in the wrong setting the same price reads as a rip-off.** The same price is gouging in a plain setting and fair in one with amenities.

How to choose (judgment): where the other side can't leave and has no alternative seller, a high opening number wasn't punished; where buyers can walk away and compare, the punishment is likely to be no deal at all rather than a lower price, and that experiment couldn't detect it. The commentator also says it cannot rule out a backlash.

## When this doesn't work

- The buyer buys often, knows the field and remembers prices: a number can pull them only a little, and a price rise is noticed immediately.
- The buyer can compare prices directly or has substitutes: the experimental subjects mostly couldn't, so how much of the effect is left is unknown.
- Most of the results cited are ratings, valuations or choices on paper, and watches are status goods; whether prices feel similarly compressed for functional tools or business software has no evidence.

## Examples

- In a performance market that lives on out-of-town visitors, several producers said that seats priced too low made people suspect something was wrong. One put the front seats and most boxes at the top price and said they sold out fast, while with many price points only the top one sold out and the cheap seats stayed empty. Meanwhile a public open-air venue kept its farthest tier extremely cheap for years; it often drew no buyers, while its priciest seats were hard to get. Lesson: this rests only on their own accounts and the commentator's interpretation, with no controlled comparison; "sold out fast" may simply mean the price was too low, and seat distance or ticketing channels were not ruled out. Use it to examine your own cheapest tier.
- A beer-choice experiment: business-school students chose between a premium and a budget brand, and twice as many picked the premium one. Adding something cheaper and worse that nobody wanted raised the budget brand's share from about a third to nearly half; adding something pricier and only a bit better drew about a tenth, and nearly everyone else picked the original premium brand.
- After a large brewer pushed an ultra-premium new product hard, its main brand rose to number one and total sales climbed together; the researcher who wrote the decoy-effect paper explained afterwards that the new product made the main brand look less expensive. There are no sales figures and no other cause was ruled out, so treat it as a story consistent with the effect, not a test.

## How solid is the evidence

- Numerical anchoring (compare, then estimate a quantity): in a later multi-lab replication every question showed clear anchoring, with larger effects than the original, making this one of the firmest blocks in the card. But it measures estimating a quantity, not an irrelevant number on a pricing page, and the specific figures in the sources are secondhand and were not checked against the original paper.
- Irrelevant numbers pulling willingness to pay: in the original auction experiment the high-number group bid more than three times as much as the low-number group. When the design was re-run later, only very weak anchoring turned up for ordinary goods and none for lotteries, so don't expect that magnitude.
- The third option (decoy): the original subjects were students. A later systematic review of 38 studies found the effect appears reliably only when attributes are shown as numbers, and disappears when buyers actually experience the product or get sensory information such as images; in another large batch of attempts, only eleven of more than ninety produced a reliable effect.
- Gains and losses relative to a reference point: in risky-choice tasks, a later replication across nearly twenty countries and over four thousand people reproduced most of the effects. But whether losses weigh more overall in everyday, riskless price reactions is disputed, and one review found the evidence unsupportive; the loss-aversion coefficient of 1.5 to 2.5 is only an average and varies by person.
- Feeling compressed relative to the amount: one controlled experiment plus a few classroom exercises, with no systematic replication mentioned in the sources. Price as a quality cue, the same beer in different settings and the supermarket price-rise order are practitioners' accounts, one thought experiment and the commentator's observation. The absence of punishment for high opening claims comes from two mock-jury experiments, one of them a hypothetical case put to students.
- The sources file anchoring under a broad heading about unconscious cues. Within that heading, the layer claiming that environmental cues change behavior (seeing certain images makes people selfish, and the like) was later overturned by independent replication, and the commentator publicly retracted it; number estimation is a different kind of evidence, so don't discard or keep the two together just because they share a heading.

## One-page checklist

- [ ] I've written down whether the buyer has a reference price; if not, what my first number is
- [ ] Every willingness-to-pay figure I hold is labeled with how it was obtained, and I've tried rewording the question
- [ ] Each price move has the buyer's baseline written down and a note on whether it will read as a gain or a loss
- [ ] I've considered whether my cheapest tier will read as "something's wrong"
- [ ] If I add a third tier, I plan to compare real purchases with and without it

## Not covered by this card

- What number to put on a new product: cost-plus, value-based pricing and competitor benchmarking are not covered in the sources
- How to measure willingness to pay and run price tests: controlled price tests, tier pilots, different prices for early users
- How to carry out a price rise: how much, how to talk to existing customers, how to transition them
- The long-term cost and design of discounts: whether discount frequency lowers the reference price, how to structure coupons
- Bundles, subscription ladders, free tiers, charm pricing, and how much "certainty" itself is worth in refund promises or results guarantees
- Pricing for business customers, subscriptions and software
- How much of these effects is left when buyers have substitutes and can compare; how to estimate a demand curve or price elasticity in practice
- Compliance and trust costs: unit-price labeling rules and the cost of being seen as a gouger
