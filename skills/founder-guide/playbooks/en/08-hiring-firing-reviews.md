# Hiring, Firing and Performance Reviews

Hiring, assessing and firing people often get stuck on the same thing: whether expectations and standards were made clear in the first place. The biggest flaw in reviews is, mainly, that the manager never spelled out expectations, and the first thing to ask before dismissing someone is whether you hired or managed them wrongly. The two sources differ in temperament — one gives processes and question lists, the other stresses setting the standard yourself and making the final call alone; where they diverge is covered under "Two schools of thought".

## Quick take

- **Key questions**: Have I written down my expectations of this person and told them — before I let them go, can I answer "did I tell them clearly"? What does this role need most right now, and which weaknesses am I willing to put up with? For the person I want to replace, can the problem be traced back to my own job definition, hiring or onboarding?
- **Preferred plays**: (1) Hiring: write down the abilities you want and the weaknesses you'll tolerate, leave about 80% of the interview time for them to talk, check references against the same standard, and make the final call yourself; (2) What to assess: write your expectations down and tell them; there's no law for weighting output against process, but you should be able to say which two variables you are trading off; (3) Whether to let an executive go: look for root causes rather than just "poor performance" — was the job clearly defined, did you help them integrate, has the company grown to about 4 times its size — then win the board's support one member at a time and announce it the same day, in order.
- **Common traps**: Reviews that don't state expectations, with vague comments, no word on how to improve and too many surprises; making exceptions for convenience (two people jointly running one role, skipping assessment and feedback, overcompensating someone who has an offer from elsewhere), which often come back later with interest; dismissing an executive and citing only poor performance, and in layoffs describing the reason as assessment and pushing the dismissals onto others.
- **Evidence**: All of it comes from two senior managers' personal experience and a few cases they picked themselves, with no comparison data; the interview question list has never been tested for predictive accuracy, and "dismissing an executive means you hired wrong" and "the four hiring criteria" are strong claims whose only evidence is the practitioner's own experience.
- **Not in this card**: Choosing who to lay off, how many, how much compensation, legal procedure and notice periods; what to do when the person to be replaced is a co-founder.

## Ask these first

1. Have I written down my expectations of this person and told them? Before I let them go, can I answer "did I tell them clearly"?
2. What does this role need most right now, and which weaknesses am I willing to put up with?
3. Am I rating their performance, or have I been swayed by an image like "worldly" or "high potential"?
4. When the numbers look good but turnover is high and complaints are many, do the numbers reflect activity from this period, or from earlier?
5. For the person I want to replace, can the problem be traced back to my own job definition, hiring or onboarding?
6. When they don't accept the feedback, do I want them to agree with me, or to be willing to act?

## Plays

### 1. Hiring: set the standard first, then interview, then verify, then decide yourself

Interviewing carries high risk and a high failure rate; one source says outright that however careful you are, it only improves your luck, while the other treats working out what you want as the most important step in all of hiring.
1. First get clear on what this person will do in their first month, why you expect them to join, and whether you want them to expand the team right away or to hire just one or two people next year. If you've never done the function, ideally do it yourself for a while; you can also have someone who does it brilliantly talk to candidates and sit in on interviews, but the decision can't be handed to an expert.
2. Write down the abilities you want and the weaknesses you're willing to tolerate. One source's four criteria: whether they are top in the function; business sense; whether they can make a major contribution to the company's direction; and whether they are effective within the team — weighted according to the role. Don't go on surface impressions, don't look for an imagined perfect candidate, and don't hire someone because they "have no weaknesses".
3. In the interview you control the room but leave about 80% of the time for them to talk; steer the conversation to subjects you both know, and ask them to describe what they did, why they did it that way, and what they'd change if they did it again; if they wander off topic, interrupt, apologise and change the subject.
4. Use your questions to gather four kinds of information: expertise and skills; the abilities they apply; the gap between knowing and doing (what they learned from failures, problems in their current job); and work values. You can ask them directly to rate their own level and listen to the wording and body language; if you don't know their field, first describe a process you know, then have them work through a problem using their expertise. Letting them ask questions shows how well prepared they are, but it is no safeguard — someone who had memorised the annual report and asked sharp questions performed terribly once hired.
5. Verify: the founder personally checks references against the same standard; talking to people the candidate didn't mention is highly effective, and what you're checking is whether they meet the standard, not whether the feedback is positive or negative; with references you don't know, lengthen the call and look for shared experiences.
6. Use two interview panels: one judges whether the candidate meets the standard, the other whether they will fit in and whose support will be key once they are hired; discuss after each interview to align everyone's understanding of the standard. In the end you decide for yourself — a unanimous decision almost always favours the person with no weaknesses.
7. When hiring a manager from a big company, watch for two kinds of mismatch: pace (used to waiting for emails and scheduled meetings) and skills (good at optimising within an existing organisation, not at building processes from scratch). In the interview ask three things: what will you do in your first month (beware of people who stress only learning and waiting to be assigned work); how does this job differ from your current one; and why join a small company (beware if the main motive is equity). After they join, have a daily meeting with questions in hand; if they can't come up with any questions, consider replacing them (this is a harsh rule, so first check whether you've given them enough information).
- Done when: you can write out the standard and the weaknesses you'll tolerate, and every criterion is backed up by a question or a reference.

### 2. What to assess: write expectations first, then weigh output against process

1. Write down your expectations first and tell them; for managers, state plainly what you expect of them (one-on-ones, giving feedback, training staff, buying into the team's goals).
2. Look at two kinds of thing: output (results that can be charted) and process (whether they squeezed people for the immediate goal, whether they developed people, whether they handled the chores that keep the department running). There's no law for the weighting — it might be 50:50 or 90:10, and can differ from this month to next — but you should be able to say which two variables you are trading off.
3. Output figures often reflect earlier activity; in the source's example the lag was about a year. When the numbers look good but turnover is high and complaints are many, don't let the numbers persuade you.
4. When assessing a manager, look at both the person and their department, and at what they did for their reports; assess performance, not potential, and don't rate them higher than their department's performance.
5. Don't assume a small company doesn't need assessment: any organisation that cares about operating performance needs it, even if it has only two assistants.
- Done when: you can say how much weight output and process each carry, and the rating is no higher than their department's performance.

### 3. The review conversation: held to improve their future performance

The conversation is not a second reading of the written report, nor a dump of every fact you've observed; more is not better.
1. You write the review yourself; their self-assessment, written first, is for reference only. Draw on progress against plan, each quarter's target attainment, and your one-on-one meeting notes.
2. Take a blank sheet and, without prioritising yet, write down everything related to their performance; then look for relationships between the items (different faces of the same problem, why a weakness can't be shaken off, how a strength can be preserved). Those relationships are the messages to convey, and each must be backed by something they actually did.
3. Cut it down to the few points they can remember in one sitting (list 7 and they will take in only 4; the rest is wasted breath). Even if it will surprise them, say it if it helps them improve; for strong performers, also write improvement suggestions, comparing what they did with a better approach worked out afterwards.
4. Give them the written draft before the conversation: if you give it after, they will bargain over what they didn't hear; if during, they rush ahead to read it and can't concentrate.
5. In the conversation, be candid, listen, and set yourself aside: praising someone face to face is no easier than criticising them; look at them and watch their tone and physical reactions until you're sure they understand; put your own unease and guilt to one side.
- Done when: their reaction convinces you they've understood, and they know what to change next.

### 4. They won't accept it or change: aim for action, not agreement

At bottom, this kind of conversation uses conflict to solve a performance problem; unless they change completely, in the end you should ask them to leave.
1. Check yourself first: were the expectations clear, and did you give feedback? If not, fix that first, or you won't be able to answer "did I tell them clearly".
2. Work out which stage they're at: ignoring it, denying it, blaming others, taking responsibility, or looking for solutions (the stage breakdown is reconstructed from the source's description, and the original diagram may divide them differently). Use evidence so they can no longer pretend it isn't happening; while they are still denying or blaming, don't rush to solutions, and don't get ahead of them.
3. If they are willing to act, accept that, even if you aren't satisfied. In the source's example, the evaluator first tried desperately to persuade the other person; the other said he had already agreed to do it anyway, and the evaluator realised that all he had been after was his own peace of mind.
4. Use your authority only when they're stuck on blaming others, and say so openly: you know they don't agree, perhaps they are right, but for the company's sake you need them to do it; afterwards still seek their commitment and watch how they perform.
5. If they neither agree nor change, the source gives no follow-up steps; the closest it comes is asking them to leave.

### 5. Whether to let an executive go

1. First look for root causes; don't just use "poor performance" as the reason. Was the job's responsibility clearly defined? Did you hire on gut feeling? Did you value strengths, or the absence of weaknesses? Did you hire to a standard higher than current needs? Did you typecast the role? Do their ambitions run against the company's goals? Did you help them integrate? Has the company grown to about 4 times its original size (in which case the job has become an entirely new one, and it's neither incompetence nor a system error)?
2. Tell the board: phone members one by one first to win their support, get a severance package approved (higher for an executive than for ordinary staff), and protect the departing person's reputation; don't announce it suddenly at a board meeting, especially if a director recommended them.
3. The conversation: write out or rehearse what you'll say; recheck that their written evaluation matches the data you hold; make the reason clear, the tone decisive (say "I have decided", not "I think") and the compensation specific; ideally let them decide how it is announced.
4. Announce it the same day, in order: direct reports, other executives, the whole company, ideally within an hour or two; arrange the reporting lines first, it is generally sensible for you to cover their role in the interim; frame it positively and don't disparage them. Afterwards, review and improve your hiring.

### 6. Layoffs: six steps

This applies to layoffs because the company missed its plan; if the only reason is individual performance, that's a different matter.
1. First, keep a clear head: some employees may decide you're either lying or a fool, and carrying that pressure is part of the job.
2. Once you've decided, act quickly and keep the news from leaking; otherwise managers get questioned by employees and can only plead ignorance, lie, or stay silent.
3. Give only one reason: the company didn't meet its plan. Don't dress it up as using the occasion to assess performance; a single layoff can collapse "just trust me" trust, and the only way to rebuild is by telling the truth.
4. Train the managers: they dismiss their own people themselves, not by pushing it onto HR, a harsher colleague or an outside firm; prepare three things for each person — a brief explanation that the situation has nothing to do with their individual performance, a statement that there are too many people and the layoff is not up for negotiation, and a full command of the benefits and compensation details.
5. Before carrying it out, speak to the whole company, bearing in mind that you are speaking to those who are staying; show full respect to those being let go, but don't over-apologise.
6. Afterwards don't hide: be visible in the company, talk to people, help the laid-off carry their things, so they know you're grateful.

### 7. Your best person hands in their notice

This guidance is for someone who is committed but feels unappreciated; people leaving for money, or using a job move as an excuse to get promoted, are outside its scope.
1. Drop what you're doing right away, sit them down and let them finish, without arguing, lecturing or getting angry; the aim is to find the real reason. Don't try to change their mind on the spot — first buy time, and deliver on whatever you promised.
2. Seek help from your own boss, treat the company as a whole, and move them to another role if needed; if they already have an offer from elsewhere, help them think through their commitments to their current boss and colleagues.
3. Before you offer terms, ask: is this something I should have given anyway, or is the offer forcing it out of me? In the latter case the terms tend to spread, and the team may learn that waving an offer around is how to get a raise (this is the practitioner's imagined scenario, not statistics).

## Common traps

- Reviews that don't state expectations; vague comments, avoiding anything negative, no word on how to improve, looking only at the recent period, too many surprises.
- Finding only strengths and no improvements for the stars, and writing at length about the weak; or tweaking the format of a report's self-assessment and handing it back, so that they feel deceived.
- Making exceptions for convenience: two people jointly running one role, skipping assessment and feedback for fear of feeling like a big company, overcompensating someone who has an offer from elsewhere — all of these often come back later with interest; the practitioner also admits such trade-offs are sometimes reasonable, provided you keep a ledger of them and take responsibility.
- Dismissing an executive and citing only poor performance; in layoffs, describing the reason as assessment and pushing the dismissals onto others.

## Two schools of thought

The two practitioners differ in temperament: one gives a process and question lists to follow, the other stresses that you set the standard, weigh the weaknesses and make the final decision alone. But neither gives a formula — the former says outright that there is no law for weighting output against process and that careful interviewing only improves your luck; the latter says plainly that making decisions is lonely. They differ mainly in three places.

**Where responsibility lies when someone isn't working out.** One school: the premise is that the problem lies with the employee (it doesn't discuss the manager's own responsibility); use evidence to bring them out of ignoring, denying and blaming, willingness to act is enough, use authority only when they're stuck, and unless they change fundamentally, ask them to leave.
The other: mostly with the company; dismissing an executive usually means your own hiring or onboarding system failed, so check the job definition, the standard, integration and any change in scale first; this is the practitioner's strong claim, and the evidence is only his own experience.
A third voice: internal disagreements should be pointed out seriously while leaving room to correct; only if the other side persists in and widens the error does it become confrontation; it doesn't answer the question of who decides they are persisting in the error.

**Hiring by how you ask, or by setting the standard first.** One school: you control the interview, use your questions to gather four kinds of information, then check with references.
The other: first write down the abilities you want and the weaknesses you'll tolerate; a unanimous decision almost always picks the person with no weaknesses; in the end one person decides.

**When a key person wants to leave, how to keep them.** One school: take it seriously at once, and promote or move them if necessary; this is your responsibility to the company as a whole.
The other: overcompensating to keep someone who has an offer from elsewhere spreads the terms around and amounts to taking on a debt.

How to choose (judgment, with conditions taken from the sources): First, run a self-check — were expectations clear, were training and feedback given, was the role clearly defined. If you pass, the problem still lies with them and you have the authority, take the "stages plus authority" route; if they are no longer up to the job after the company scaled, blame neither side. Second, take the conciliatory route on internal disagreements only when both sides still share a goal, are still in the same organisation, and the other side can still correct course. Third, if you know the function, you can use the four-category questions directly; if you've never done it, set the standard first and use experts as a bridge, because a question list can't tell you whether what they say is right. Fourth, if the terms are recognition or a promotion you would have given anyway, do it right away; if they are being forced out of you, the debt will come to collect.

## When this doesn't work

- There are no output figures and no indicators that build up year by year (possibly the case in teams of a dozen or so): there's no data to apply the output/process trade-off to; what to rely on when you must make a judgment without data, the source doesn't say.
- Early-stage teams without a formal pay and promotion cycle: you have to find the leverage for assessment afresh (inference).
- The problem isn't ability or performance but deception, rule-breaking or other personal conduct: the sources don't discuss it. With no board, or when the person to be replaced is a co-founder, "tell the board first" has to be replaced by another audience (inference).
- Tolerating a candidate's poor cultural fit is premised in the source on the company being in a struggling "wartime" state; when you need to expand fast, you want instead someone who has previously grown a company of that size quickly.
- The six layoff steps cover only how to say it and who says it, not choosing who to lay off, how many, compensation or legal procedure; the source reflects overseas company experience, while local labour law sets mandatory rules on notice periods, compensation and procedure, so get a separate lawyer.

## Example

- A manager's department saw sales surge and net profit rise, but staff turnover was far higher than before and subordinates often complained; the boss, won over by the good numbers, gave an excellent rating. The next year growth stalled and the department was more turbulent still, and the boss concluded afterwards that the previous year's rating had been wrong — output metrics reflect earlier activity.
- A software company hiring a head of sales interviewed twenty-odd people, none good enough, and finally hired someone whose image and résumé didn't fit and who suited the company culture poorly, against the opposition of the board and most department heads. The founder had personally run sales for several months beforehand to work out what he wanted, checked dozens of references, specifically verified the candidate's biggest weakness, and judged that the company was in a struggling "wartime" state before deciding to tolerate it. The practitioner says the outcome bore out the judgment, but it's a success he picked himself.
- A company went through three large layoffs in a row, losing several hundred people, yet kept its culture and its best people; the founder drew up the six steps afterwards. He himself says it was a special case, and one investor thinks that after repeated layoffs a company can almost never go on to great success.

## How solid is the evidence

- All of it comes from two senior managers' personal experience and a few cases they picked themselves; there is no comparison data.
- The interview question list is a consensus among a group of managers and has never been tested for predictive accuracy, and the practitioner himself misjudged two people he hired; the list of flaws in review conversations comes from asking a group of managers to recall the reviews they had received.
- The six layoff steps come from one company's three layoffs, with no comparison, so you can't tell whether the method was right or something else, such as a turnaround in the business, made the difference; "dismissing an executive means you hired wrong" and "the four hiring criteria" are strong claims whose only evidence is the practitioner's own experience.
- The big-company manager scenario and the three examples of management debt are the practitioner's own constructions, not statistics; a few figures were out of place in the source and have been reconstructed.

## One-page checklist

- [ ] My expectations of each person (especially managers) are written down and I've told them
- [ ] Before hiring, I've written down the abilities I want and the weaknesses I'll tolerate; the candidate talks for 80% of the interview; references are checked against the same standard; I make the call myself
- [ ] I write the review myself: everything on a blank sheet, find the relationships, cut it down to what they can remember; they get the written draft before the conversation
- [ ] When rating a manager I look at their department, and the rating is no higher than the department's performance; I've asked whether the output figures reflect earlier activity
- [ ] Before dismissing anyone, I've asked: do they know clearly what was expected of them? Did I hire wrongly or manage them wrongly?
- [ ] With someone who won't accept it: work out their stage first, aim for action rather than agreement, and use authority only when they are stuck on blaming others, and say so
- [ ] Replacing an executive or laying people off: tell the board one by one; announce the same day in order; for layoffs give only one reason and have managers dismiss their own people
- [ ] When a key person resigns: drop what I'm doing and hear them out, don't argue, buy time; ask whether the terms are being forced out of me
