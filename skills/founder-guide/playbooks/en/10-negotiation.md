# Negotiation

A common mistake isn't conceding too much or too little. It's both sides guarding the terms they opened with while nobody touches what those terms are really for — and, on top of that, walking in without having worked out what you'll actually do if the talks fail, so every concession or walkout is a gut call. This card comes down to three moves: steady yourself first, break each demand down into the reason behind it, and measure every offer against a fallback you have actually made real.

## Quick take

- **Key questions**: If this falls through, what is the one thing I will actually do, and is it executable today? Behind each of their terms, what are they worried about or trying to protect? What am I feeling right now — is an impulse answering for me, or am I answering for my real interests?
- **Preferred plays**: (1) Negotiate with yourself first: take one minute alone before an important meeting, picture yourself watching from a balcony during the talks, name the feeling that was triggered, and before you respond notice the fork between the impulsive reply and the considered one that actually serves you; (2) Turn their demands into reasons: write the reason you suspect next to each of their terms, or ask, making clear you're trying to understand their thinking; one reason can usually be met in several ways, and that's where new options come from; (3) Measure every offer against a fallback you've made real: list what you could do if talks fail, make the most promising one or two real (a written expression of interest, a quote, a sign-off), then compare every offer to the best of them — better, worth pursuing; worse, don't take it.
- **Common traps**: Counting your fallbacks in bulk ("worst case I'll do A, or B, or go it alone") when you can only pick one if talks collapse; treating every need of theirs as a money question, when what they're really stuck on may be face, being treated as an equal, or having something to show their superiors; looking only at the person at the table, whose boss may have given them a hard instruction.
- **Evidence**: All from experienced mediators' long practice and the examples they chose, with no comparison data; the arguments are strong, but they are not experimental results.
- **Not in this card**: What to do when they bluff, set deadlines, or play good cop / bad cop; who should name a number first, how aggressive the first offer should be, and how to taper concessions.

## Ask these first

1. If this falls through, what is the one thing I will actually do? Is it executable today, or is it just "I'll find someone else"?
2. Behind each of their terms, what are they worried about or trying to protect? Is there another way to address that same worry?
3. What decision do they think I want from them? Why haven't they made it yet — what would it cost them?
4. Who does the person across the table answer to (a boss, an investment committee, family)? Have I accounted for their concerns too?
5. What will they do if we don't agree? Are they overrating their own fallback?
6. What am I feeling right now? Is an impulse answering for me, or am I answering for my real interests?

## Plays

### 1. Negotiate with yourself first: get steady, then sit down

Once a remark or a tone sets you off, you'll usually drift into one of three reactions — hitting back, giving in, or backing away — and none of them serves your interests.
1. Before an important meeting or call, take one minute alone; on ordinary days, spend a few minutes quietly noticing the thoughts and feelings that come up.
2. During the talks, picture yourself watching the scene from a balcony: notice which line or which tone triggered you, name the feeling ("that's fear", "that's feeling insulted"), and if needed jot down what your body is doing.
3. Before you respond, notice the fork in front of you: the impulsive reply, or the considered one that actually serves you.
4. Ask yourself "why do I want this?" several times over until you reach the need underneath; very often the term you're fighting for is just one wrapper around that need.
- Done when: the other side raises their voice and you can name what you're feeling while still following your plan.

### 2. Turn their demands into reasons

A demand is a decision they've already made; the reason is why they made it. One reason can usually be met in several ways, and that's where new options come from.
1. Next to each of their terms, write the reason you suspect (insisting on yearly price adjustments may mean they fear rising costs).
2. You can also just ask — but make it clear you're trying to understand their thinking, not asking them to defend their position.
3. From their side, draw two columns: what they gain and lose if they say yes to you, and what happens if they say no — both for them personally and for the organisation they represent.
4. Check them against a few basic needs: security, money, belonging, recognition, control over their own affairs. Don't assume money is all they care about.
5. Keep a list of both sides' reasons, ranked, and update it as you learn more. Don't chase precision — you're trying to understand a human choice, not solve an equation.

### 3. When you speak, lead with the problem, then the answer

1. Be concrete about how serious your problem is — times, places, numbers, and how serious it is — so they hear an issue, not an attack on them.
2. Play back your understanding of their concerns and ask, "Have I got that right? Is there anything else?"
3. Give your reasons before your proposal. Open with terms and deadlines and they'll be busy preparing a rebuttal; your explanation won't land.
4. Don't relitigate the past; ask "who does what next?"
5. Bring more than one option, each tied to one of your needs. Be firm on the issue and courteous to the person — you need both.

### 4. Measure every offer against a fallback you've made real

Fixing a hard bottom line in advance protects you from pressure, but it also stops you from hearing new information and is easy to set too high or too low. A better yardstick: the best thing you would actually do if this deal fails.
1. List everything you could do if you don't reach agreement — the more the better.
2. Take the most promising one or two and make them real: a written expression of interest from another party, a quote from another customer, your team's sign-off on the backup plan.
3. Pick the best of them. From then on, compare every offer to it: better than your fallback, worth pursuing; worse, don't take it.
4. Also set a tripwire: when talks reach that point, you pause and go back to review with your co-founders rather than walking out automatically. This matters most when someone on your side might get talked round at the table.
5. Estimate their fallback: what they'll do if they don't deal with you, and how good it is. If they overrate it, help them see it clearly; if it's genuinely strong, look for leverage that changes it.
6. Whether to reveal your fallback depends on what they believe: if yours is better than they think, let them know; if it's worse, revealing it only weakens you.
- Done when: you can write "if this fails, I will do X" and use it to answer "is this offer better or worse than X?"

## Common traps

- Thinking there are only two ways to negotiate, soft or hard. The one who keeps conceding loses to the one who never moves, and two sides dug into their terms waste time and damage the relationship. What needs to change is the method, not where you sit between soft and hard.
- Counting your fallbacks in bulk — "worst case I'll do A, or B, or go it alone." When talks collapse you only get to pick one; you can't have all your options at once.
- Being too pessimistic when you have no fallback; the bigger risk is often wanting the deal too much.
- Treating every need of theirs as a money question, when what they're really stuck on may be face, being treated as an equal, or having something to show their superiors.
- Looking only at the person at the table: their boss may have given them a hard instruction, and they may not be free to decide.
- Trading jabs back and forth: it looks like negotiating, but both sides are only trying to score points and nobody is trying to change the other's mind.

## Two schools of thought

**One school: treat them as someone who responds to reasons.** Ask about their concerns, explain why you're asking, prepare several options, and use the reasons behind the demands to reach a new solution together.

**The other school: deal with their emotions and guardedness first.** Asking "what do you mean by that?" can put people on the defensive; it works better to repeat their last few words and wait quietly for them to elaborate. When holding a term you won't concede, say it plainly in a calm, downward-inflected voice and offer no alternatives, and stop once you've said it.

The two actually agree on one point: neither recommends simply being nice — one asks you to be firm on the problem, the other to hold the point you won't concede. "Better no deal than a bad deal" is stated outright only in the first school's sources. They split mainly (judgment) on whether the other side is driven by reason or by fear and emotion, and on whether to address interests or emotions first.

How to choose (judgment): when there's an existing relationship and they're willing to talk, ask about reasons directly and bring options. When they're guarded, emotional, or far more powerful than you, start with repetition and silence and let them open up. Options belong to your preparation; at the table, when you're holding a particular point, state one position and don't add alternatives.

## When they're stronger than you

- Negotiating power comes less from money and status than from who can better afford for the talks to fail — that is, how good each side's fallback is. Start by asking how real your fallback is, and how real theirs is.
- Against a stronger party there are only two realistic goals: don't accept terms you should refuse, and make the most of what you have. Some gaps can't be negotiated away; admit it.
- Making your fallback real is the most effective step against a stronger party (the proponent's view); improving yours without looking at theirs is only half the job.

## When this doesn't work

- Pure division of a fixed pie (valuation, equity split, discount rate; the examples in brackets are inference): digging for reasons yields few new options, and the harder side may indeed win. The sources' answer is to bring in a fair standard both sides accept (market price, convention, an outside opinion), not to dig in.
- The other side is deliberately hiding things or misleading you: asking directly and imagining their position both fail, and the sources behind this card don't deal with liars.
- Your fallback is hard to turn into something comparable (will the next round close? will another buyer appear?): comparing offers against it gets fuzzy (inference).
- Making a fallback real costs time and resources; when the negotiating window is short, the sources don't say how to trade that off.
- Techniques that rely on tone of voice (a downward-inflected statement, repeating and then staying silent) work only face to face or on the phone; email and text have no tone to use, and the source doesn't say how to get the same effect in writing.
- A one-off counterpart who only wants to squeeze the price: leading with reasons and acknowledging their concerns may just waste time or tip your hand (inference).
- When your side has several people and you need to bind whoever is negotiating, or stop your coalition from being split, a fixed bottom line can actually be useful.

## Example

- A founder and a partner who had bought in later were locked in arbitration and litigation for more than two years. At their first meeting the mediator didn't discuss a single term; he kept asking what the founder really wanted. What finally came out wasn't "to win" but to be free to go and do other things. By the mediator's own account, once that was clear the long-running dispute was settled within days. The source gives only the outcome, not the negotiation itself, so treat this as an illustration of negotiating with yourself first, not as a repeatable procedure.

## How solid is the evidence

- "Steady yourself", "turn demands into reasons" and "measure offers against your fallback" come from experienced mediators' long practice and the examples they chose; there's no comparison data. The arguments are strong, but they are not experimental results.
- "In most negotiations shared interests outnumber opposing ones" rests on a handful of chosen examples, mostly of the kind where interests separate easily (rentals, neighbours, diplomacy).
- "The stronger the other side, the more you gain by sticking to principle" is supported only by a metaphor — don't lean on it.
- Repeating the last few words and holding a line in a downward tone come from frontline crisis negotiation, mostly one-off, high-pressure, highly emotional situations.

## One-page checklist

- [ ] I've written "if this fails, I will do X", and X is real (a written expression of interest, a quote, or a sign-off)
- [ ] I've set a tripwire: at that point I pause and go back to discuss
- [ ] Each of their terms has a suspected reason next to it, and I've checked all five basic needs
- [ ] I've estimated their fallback and decided whether to reveal mine
- [ ] I have more than one option, each tied to one of my needs
- [ ] Opening order: problem and reasons first, proposal second
- [ ] I took a minute beforehand and know which line is most likely to set me off
